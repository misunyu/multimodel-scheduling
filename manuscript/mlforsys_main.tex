\begin{abstract}
Placing concurrent inference workloads on heterogeneous nodes is difficult because isolated profiles do not capture co-execution interference. We present a learning-based method that predicts system-level performance from offline profiles and ranks placements without online execution. Under our platform-wise group-normalized objective, the GPU and NPU select different best measured placements in 11 of 45 workload groups, including 10 involving generative models. Motivated by this platform-dependent behavior, we compare predictor performance under three settings: platform-specific training, zero-shot cross-platform transfer, and joint training on both platforms. Zero-shot transfer reduces ranking quality, whereas joint training approaches platform-specific ranking performance, matching platform-specific Top-1 accuracy at 93.3\% on the GPU and 86.7\% on the NPU. These results show that target-platform co-execution profiles improve cross-platform ranking, while a single jointly trained predictor can serve both platforms.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}


Modern inference nodes serve diverse workloads on shared accelerators, including vision models, large language models (LLMs), and vision-language models (VLMs). Under co-execution, workloads contend for accelerator and host resources, making isolated profiles unreliable for predicting the best placement under load. Online evaluation is also impractical because the candidate space grows exponentially with the number of workloads, making exhaustive search too costly for each workload configuration.

Learning-based models predict performance under contention~\citep{kim_iccd2024_interference,xu_tpds2023_igniter,cui2021}, but prior predictors are developed and evaluated on a single platform, leaving unclear whether they capture workload characteristics or platform-specific behavior. We study this question on an NVIDIA RTX~5090 GPU and a Mobilint MLA100 NPU~\citep{mobilint_mla100} under controlled workloads and system configurations. Using offline co-execution profiles, we predict system-level performance and rank placements without online measurement. This controlled comparison lets us assess whether target-platform co-execution data improve placement ranking.


\textbf{Contributions.}  We make three main contributions: (i) We formulate and evaluate placement ranking from offline profiles without online candidate execution. (ii) We conduct a controlled comparison of CPU--GPU and CPU--NPU platforms, finding platform-dependent best placements, degraded zero-shot transfer, and near-platform-specific ranking with joint training. (iii) We show that direct regression achieves higher ranking accuracy than a capacity-based heuristic and a decomposed per-instance model, with a statistically significant Top-1 gain over the decomposed model on the NPU.





\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Offline profiling collects platform-specific measurements and trains the predictors. At deployment time, the predictors rank feasible placements without online execution.

\textbf{Placement model.} A node contains a CPU and one accelerator. We denote the available devices by $\mathcal{D}=\{\mathrm{CPU},\mathrm{ACC}\}$, where $\mathrm{ACC}$ is either the GPU or the NPU. The node serves $N$ concurrent application instances. Each instance is an end-to-end pipeline whose auxiliary stages execute on the CPU. A placement $\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$, with $x_i\in\mathcal{D}$, assigns the core model of each instance to a device. 
% The VLM is restricted to the accelerator. 
Placements are selected before deployment and remain fixed at runtime.

\textbf{Performance objectives.} For a workload--placement pair, we define aggregate vision throughput $r_1$, mean deadline miss rate $r_2$, and total token rate $r_3$ over all scheduled generative instances. Response time includes queueing delay, and late, dropped, or incomplete requests count as misses when computing $r_2$. The measurement window and concrete deadline setting are specified in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\textbf{Normalization and ranking.} A workload group contains all candidate placements for the same model set and request rate configuration. Within each group, $r_1$ and $r_3$ are divided by their maxima, while $r_2$ is min--max scaled to $[0,1]$. We denote the normalized targets by $y_1$, $y_2$, and $y_3$. Candidates are ranked using
\begin{equation}
S(\mathbf{x}) = y_1(\mathbf{x}) - \alpha y_2(\mathbf{x})
+ \beta y_3(\mathbf{x}),
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \ge 0$ and $\beta \ge 0$ are operator-set trade-off weights rather than learned parameters. The $\beta$ term is omitted when a workload has no generative model. At deployment, the three regressors directly predict $\hat y_1$, $\hat y_2$, and $\hat y_3$ for every feasible candidate. Equation~\eqref{eq:score} uses these predictions to rank candidates without computing target-group normalization statistics. 
Because normalization is group-specific, scores are comparable only within a group.
%Because normalization is group-specific, the score supports only within-group ranking and cannot establish whether an absolute deadline-miss threshold is satisfied.


\section{Methods and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Methods Under Comparison}
\label{sec:methods}

% We compare our direct model with two baselines and a measured oracle.

\textbf{Direct system-level regression.} Each placement is represented by features that summarize request rates, model types, device assignments, isolated device profiles, and aggregate load. Three XGBoost regressors~\citep{chen2016_xgboost} predict the normalized targets $\hat y_1$, $\hat y_2$, and $\hat y_3$ for each candidate placement. Substituting these predictions for $y_1$, $y_2$, and $y_3$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:score} yields the predicted score $\hat S(\mathbf{x})$, and candidates are ranked in descending order of $\hat S(\mathbf{x})$.

\textbf{Capacity greedy baseline.} This non-learning baseline uses the same request, placement, and isolated-profile information as our predictor, but assumes additive resource demand. It scans instances in decreasing order of isolated accelerator speedup and assigns an instance to the accelerator when its estimated load fits the remaining capacity. Otherwise, the instance is assigned to the CPU and the scan continues. 


\textbf{Decomposed per-instance model.} This baseline adapts prior per-task degradation prediction~\citep{kim_iccd2024_interference}. A shared regressor predicts each vision instance's attainment under contention from its configuration and a summary of its co-runners. Attainment is achieved throughput divided by the smaller of the request rate and isolated capacity. The instance predictions are composed into $\hat y_1$ and $\hat y_2$ using measured evaluation-group normalization statistics unavailable to our direct predictor at deployment. The generative target $\hat y_3$ uses the same predictor as the direct model.

\textbf{Measured oracle.} The oracle treats every candidate tied for the highest measured score $S$ in a workload group as correct.

\subsection{Testbed and Workloads}

\textbf{Platforms.} Both platforms share the same Intel Core Ultra 9 285K host, workload definitions, and measurement procedure. The GPU platform uses an NVIDIA RTX~5090, while the NPU platform uses a Mobilint MLA100~\citep{mobilint_mla100}. Each accelerator uses its native software stack. On the MLA100, core assignment serves as a scheduling hint rather than an exclusive reservation. Multiple models can reside on the device, and all measured placements fit in device memory. Thus, the VLM pinning described in Appendix~A is the only device-assignment restriction. Detailed execution settings and software stacks appear in Appendix~G.


\textbf{Workloads.}
Nine models---YOLO11 n/s/m/l/x, ResNet50, MobileNet-v2, the LLaMA-3.2-1B LLM, and the Qwen2-VL-2B VLM---form 15 workload sets with two to eight concurrent instances. The sets vary in concurrency, the presence of generative models, and the detector variant, and include vision-only controls. Each set is evaluated at three request-rate levels.


\subsection{Measurement and Evaluation Protocol}

\textbf{Measurement and target construction.} Each workload--placement pair uses a 20\,s warm-up followed by a 180\,s measurement window. Each vision stream's response deadline is three times its request interval and therefore tightens as the request rate increases. For 12 CPU-LLM windows per platform, per-instance token attribution is unavailable, so we set $y_3=0$. Restoring the recorded window totals changes neither Top-1 outcomes nor divergence labels (Appendix~B).

\textbf{Candidate sets and data collection.} We construct a feasible candidate set for each workload set. Ten sets are measured exhaustively. For the remaining five, we group candidate placements by the number of unpinned instances assigned to the accelerator and sample a prespecified number from each group.
Both platforms evaluate the same candidate placements. Across the 15 sets, this yields 180 placement configurations. Evaluating each configuration at three request-rate levels produces 540 measurement windows and 45 workload groups per platform. Data collection required approximately 32 hours per platform. Appendix~A reports per-set coverage.

\textbf{Evaluation protocol.} We use three-fold group-wise cross-validation, assigning each workload group to a single fold. This holds out all placements in the group, although other request-rate levels of the same model set may remain in training. Targets are normalized within each workload group. Normalization statistics computed on held-out groups are used only to construct evaluation targets. For joint training, paired GPU and NPU measurements from the same workload group share the same fold. Unless otherwise stated, all ranking and divergence analyses use $\alpha{=}0.3$ and $\beta{=}1.0$. Appendix~F gives the training settings and feature schema. Appendix~C reports sensitivity to both coefficients.




\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\textbf{Ranking quality.} Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarizes ranking performance under group-wise validation. Direct regression reaches group-mean Spearman $0.986/0.972$ and Top-1 $0.933/0.867$ (GPU/NPU). In every workload group, the predictor's top-ranked placement attains at least $91\%$ of the best measured score. The corresponding mean ratios are $99.6\%$ on the GPU and $99.3\%$ on the NPU. Appendix~B reports per-set results. 
%Unless otherwise stated, subsequent analyses use the same validation protocol and measured candidate set.

\textbf{Best measured placements differ across platforms.} Under the normalized objective in Equation~\eqref{eq:score}, the best measured placement differs between the GPU and NPU in 11 of 45 workload groups, including 10 of 33 generative groups and one of 12 vision-only groups (Figure~\ref{fig:divergence}). At $\beta{=}0.5$, the number of disagreements increases to 16 of 45. Among the 30 exhaustively measured groups, 8 of 24 generative groups differ, while no divergence occurs in the 6 vision-only groups. The resulting disagreement rate of $26.7\%$ is no lower than the $24.4\%$ rate over all 45 measured groups, which argues against incomplete candidate sampling as an explanation for the observed pattern (Appendix~A). Sensitivity to the scoring coefficients is also platform-dependent. Across the sweep with $\beta{>}0$, the best-placement set differs from the baseline in at most three workload sets on the GPU but in as many as nine on the NPU (Appendix~C).

\textbf{Transfer across platforms.} Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} compares platform-specific, zero-shot, and joint training. The zero-shot predictor is trained on one platform and evaluated on the other without retraining or target-platform co-execution data, while retaining target-platform isolated profiles as features. The joint predictor pools data from both platforms without a separate platform identifier. Platform information enters through device-assignment indicators and isolated-profile values. On the NPU, zero-shot transfer reduces Spearman correlation from $0.972$ to $0.907$ and Top-1 accuracy from $0.867$ to $0.800$. On the GPU, Spearman correlation decreases from $0.986$ to $0.927$, while Top-1 accuracy remains $0.933$. Joint training largely closes the zero-shot gap, reaching Spearman $0.975/0.984$ and Top-1 $0.933/0.867$ (GPU/NPU), matching platform-specific Top-1 on both platforms.

\textbf{Comparison with simpler models.} The capacity-based greedy baseline achieves Top-1 accuracies of $0.424$ on the GPU and $0.333$ on the NPU when evaluated on the 33 workload groups with measured outputs. It uses the same input information as direct regression but relies on additive capacity accounting, and the gap highlights the limitations of this heuristic under non-additive contention (Appendix~D). The decomposed model achieves pooled instance-level Spearman correlations of $0.993/0.992$ but placement-level Top-1 accuracies of $0.911/0.622$ (GPU/NPU). Appendix~E shows that most of the placement-level ranking error arises in the composition step rather than per-instance prediction. Direct regression achieves higher Top-1 accuracy than the decomposed model, but a cluster bootstrap over workload sets finds the difference statistically significant only on the NPU (Appendix~H).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Prior serving systems optimize request execution and resource allocation at cluster scale~\citep{crankshaw_nsdi2017_clipper,shen_sosp2019_nexus,li_osdi2023_alpaserve,gujarati_osdi2020_clockwork}, while intra-node methods schedule layers or operators across heterogeneous processors~\citep{oh_hpca2021_layerweaver,dagli_ppopp2024_haxconn,omniboost2025,yu2023partituner}. Neither targets node-local CPU--accelerator assignment at the application-instance level. Learned interference prediction is closest to our work. Abacus~\citep{cui2021} predicts latency under GPU operator overlap, whereas iGniter~\citep{xu_tpds2023_igniter} models interference for GPU resource provisioning. \citet{kim_iccd2024_interference} predict per-task interference for runtime scheduling on heterogeneous edge processors, and our decomposed baseline adapts this structure. HEFT uses task-graph costs, while RL-based placement learns from execution-derived rewards~\citep{topcuoglu2002heft,mirhoseini2017_deviceplacement}. We instead rank fixed placements on CPU--GPU and CPU--NPU nodes from offline co-execution profiles.

\paragraph{Limitations and conclusion.}
Our evaluation covers only measured candidates and excludes runtime re-placement. Set-dependent request rates limit cross-set comparisons, and group-normalized $y_2$ cannot establish absolute deadline compliance. Native stacks and precisions differ, so the observed differences are platform-level rather than hardware-only. Within these limits, best measured placements can differ across platforms, and joint training matches platform-specific Top-1 on both platforms. Direct regression achieves higher Top-1 than both baselines, although its gain over the decomposed model is statistically significant only on the NPU.

\subsection*{A. Candidate-set coverage by workload set}
This appendix characterizes candidate-placement coverage for each workload set and examines whether the observed GPU--NPU placement divergence is an artifact of incomplete sampling.

The same feasibility rules apply to profiling, evaluation, and deployment. Each model may run on any device permitted by its execution constraints, while the VLM is pinned to the accelerator. Its isolated CPU time to first token is $12.3$\,s, against $0.10$\,s on the GPU and $0.61$\,s on the NPU, so a single 32-token request takes about $18$\,s on the CPU. A CPU-placed VLM would therefore run almost continuously through a 180\,s window and, because generative workers use the default thread count (Appendix~G), would hold host CPU for its duration. We exclude these placements from the candidate space. Profiling subsamples the stratified sets to bound collection cost, whereas deployment enumerates the full feasible set. Evaluation uses only the profiled placements.

The five workload sets labeled base1--base5 each contain LLaMA-3.2-1B, Qwen2-VL-2B, ResNet50, and one YOLO11 detector. They differ only in the detector variant, using YOLO11 n, s, m, l, and x, respectively. The remaining sets, S1--S10, vary in concurrency, generative-model composition, and request-rate configuration.

Table~\ref{tab:app_coverage} reports the full placement space, the feasible subset, and the measured subset for each workload set. Because each VLM is pinned to the accelerator, a set with $N$ instances has $2^{N-\#\mathrm{VLM}}$ feasible placements. Sets without a VLM retain all $2^N$ placements. We measure ten workload sets exhaustively. For the remaining five, we stratify placements by the number of non-pinned instances assigned to the accelerator and sample each stratum using quotas fixed before data collection. Both platforms use the same generated measurement schedule, and post-collection verification confirms that their measured candidate sets are identical for every workload set and request-rate configuration.

All appendix analyses follow the normalization procedure in Section~\ref{sec:problem}. Within each workload group, $y_1=r_1/\max r_1$ and $y_3=r_3/\max r_3$, while $y_2=(r_2-\min r_2)/(\max r_2-\min r_2)$, with $y_2{=}0$ when all candidates have the same $r_2$. For the 12 CPU-only LLM windows per platform described in Appendix~B, $y_3$ is imputed as zero rather than computed from the recorded window total. We use a tie-aware definition of the best measured placement. Every candidate whose score is within $10^{-9}$ of the group maximum is included in the best-placement set. The two platforms are considered to agree when their best-placement sets intersect.

Among the 30 exhaustively measured workload groups, the GPU and NPU disagree on the best measured placement in 8 groups: 8 of 24 generative groups and none of the 6 vision-only groups. At $\beta{=}0.5$, 13 of the 30 exhaustive groups diverge. The corresponding rates are $26.7\%$ against $24.4\%$ over all 45 groups at $\beta{=}1.0$, and $43.3\%$ against $35.6\%$ at $\beta{=}0.5$. The sampled vision-only controls S9 and S10 account for only one divergence among their six workload groups, occurring for S10 at its highest request rate. These comparisons provide no evidence that incomplete sampling inflates the observed divergence rate.

\subsection*{B. Per-set out-of-fold ranking fidelity}
Table~\ref{tab:app_perset} reports the out-of-fold Spearman correlation for each target and workload set, averaged across the request-rate levels. The $y_3$ regressor is trained only on the 372 windows per platform in which a generative instance runs on the accelerator. The remaining 168 windows consist of 156 vision-only windows, for which $y_3$ is not applicable, and 12 windows in which the LLM runs on the CPU. For these 12 windows, recorded window totals range from $0.0$ to $2.02$ tok/s, but per-generative-instance token attribution was unavailable in the retained data. The target-construction pipeline therefore imputes $y_3{=}0$, which is an imputation convention rather than a measured zero. All 168 windows are excluded from $y_3$ training.

The trained regressor nevertheless scores these candidates, producing extrapolated $\hat{y}_3$ values for a placement regime absent from training. Because S3 contains a generative model, the $\beta$ term remains active for every candidate, so these extrapolated predictions enter the final placement scores.

For all three S3 rate groups on both platforms, the platform-specific predictor's top-ranked candidate belongs to the best-placement set. Under these out-of-fold predictors, extrapolated $\hat{y}_3$ values for these candidates range from $0.12$ to $0.26$ on the GPU and from $0.90$ to $0.98$ on the NPU. After restoring and renormalizing the recorded window totals, the corresponding measured $y_3$ values remain at most $0.017$ on the GPU and $0.121$ on the NPU because accelerator-side LLM candidates determine the within-group maxima.

We test two interventions for four predictors on each platform, yielding eight predictor--platform combinations. The four predictors are the platform-specific, zero-shot, joint, and decomposed models. Neither setting the extrapolated $\hat{y}_3$ values to zero nor replacing the imputed labels with renormalized recorded window totals changes any Top-1 outcome. The label replacement also preserves the same 11 workload groups classified as cross-platform divergences. The largest absolute change in group-mean Spearman correlation is $0.013$, attained by the decomposed predictor on the NPU.

The $y_3$ target applies only to sets containing a generative model. The two lowest $y_3$ correlations, observed for S3-GPU and S4-NPU, come from $N{=}3$ sets with few valid $y_3$ candidates. A single rank inversion therefore changes the correlation substantially.

\subsection*{C. Sensitivity to scoring coefficients}

Table~\ref{tab:app_sweep} evaluates how the best measured placements change as the scoring coefficients vary. For each $(\alpha,\beta)$ pair, it reports the number of workload sets whose best-placement set differs from the baseline at $\alpha{=}0.3$ and $\beta{=}1.0$. A workload set is counted as changed if, for at least one of its three request-rate groups, the tie-aware best-placement set is disjoint from the corresponding baseline set. Rate groups are compared separately rather than merged, using the $10^{-9}$ tie tolerance of Appendix~A. The comparison covers all 15 workload sets.

When the generative objective is active with $\beta{>}0$, the two platforms exhibit different levels of sensitivity. The GPU best-placement set changes in at most three workload sets, and for $\alpha{\ge}0.3$ with $\beta{>}0$ the only set that changes is S8. In contrast, the NPU best-placement set changes in as many as nine sets, primarily among the base workloads, S5, and S7.

The $\beta{=}0$ setting removes the generative objective entirely and causes substantial changes on both platforms. We therefore include this setting for completeness but exclude it from the cross-platform sensitivity comparison. For any fixed $\beta{>}0$, varying only $\alpha$ changes the best-placement set in at most one set on the GPU and up to seven sets on the NPU. These results indicate greater placement sensitivity on the NPU than on the GPU across the evaluated coefficient sweep. The asymmetry is more pronounced at the workload-group level. At $(\alpha,\beta){=}(0,0.25)$, where both platform maxima occur, the GPU best-placement set changes in 3 of 45 workload groups and the NPU best-placement set in 20 of 45. The NPU count exceeds the GPU count in 19 of the 20 coefficient settings with $\beta{>}0$.

\subsection*{D. Greedy ordering variants and unmeasured placements}

Although the speedup ordering produces more unmeasured placements, it achieves higher Top-1 accuracy over measured outputs on both platforms. \subsection*{E. Composition error in the decomposed model}

Table~\ref{tab:app_decomp} examines whether the performance loss of the decomposed model arises from per-instance prediction or from composing instance-level estimates into system-level metrics. The shared regressor predicts individual attainment accurately when all instance-level samples are pooled.

Let $\hat a_i$ denote the predicted attainment of vision instance $i$, let $a_i=\min(1, t_i/e_i)$ be its measured attainment with achieved throughput $t_i$, and let $e_i=\min(\lambda_i,c_i)$ be its effective request rate, where $\lambda_i$ is the assigned rate and $c_i$ the isolated capacity. Writing $\mathcal{V}$ for the vision instances of a placement, the composition is
\[
  \hat y_1 = \frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}} \hat a_i\, e_i}{\max_{g} r_1},
  \qquad
  \hat y_2 = \frac{m - \min_{g} r_2}{\max_{g} r_2 - \min_{g} r_2},
  \quad
  m = \mathrm{clip}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}} (1-\hat a_i),\, 0,\, 1\Big),
\]
with $\hat y_2{=}0$ when the group's $r_2$ range vanishes, and $\min_g$, $\max_g$ taken over the measured candidates of the evaluation group. The throughput composition weights each instance by $e_i$, whereas the deadline-miss composition is an unweighted mean. Although generative co-runners are included in the per-instance features, their effects are modeled separately for each vision instance and are not explicitly represented during system-level composition. The generative target $\hat y_3$ is taken unchanged from the direct predictor's out-of-fold output.

To isolate miss-rate composition error, we recompose the miss rate from measured rather than predicted attainments. This removes per-instance prediction error from the diagnostic. The comparison is made on the raw miss-rate scale before group normalization, so no evaluation-group statistic enters it. Under measured attainments the throughput reconstruction is exact: the attainment target is never clipped in our data, so $a_i e_i = t_i$ for every instance and $\sum_i a_i e_i$ reproduces the measured aggregate throughput. This diagnostic therefore isolates composition error in the mapping from per-instance attainment to the aggregate miss rate.

Recomposing the miss rate from measured attainments still reduces ranking fidelity. The deadline-miss composition uses an unweighted mean and therefore ignores differences in effective request rates. It also cannot represent residual cross-instance effects that are not captured by the individual attainment predictions. The final row of Table~\ref{tab:app_decomp} reports the placement-level ranking performance of the fully predicted composition, which uses measured evaluation-group statistics and therefore favors the decomposed baseline. Top-5 accuracy---the fraction of groups in which a best measured placement appears among the five highest-ranked candidates---is $1.000$ on both platforms, but the lower Top-1 accuracy shows that the combined prediction and composition errors can prevent identification of the best measured placement.

\subsection*{F. Training configuration and feature schema}
For each outer fold and each target, we select the tree depth $\texttt{max\_depth}$ and the learning rate $\eta$ from four candidate pairs: $(3, 0.1)$, $(4, 0.1)$, $(5, 0.05)$, and $(6, 0.1)$. Selection uses inner three-fold group-wise cross-validation restricted to the outer-fold training rows. The inner and outer splits use the same grouping key, and the pair with the lowest mean absolute error is selected. All remaining settings are fixed to the squared-error objective, 300 boosting rounds, a subsampling rate of $0.8$, a column-sampling rate of $0.8$, a minimum child weight of $3$, and seed~42. The same training procedure is used for the platform-specific, joint, and decomposed models. The outer split follows the three-fold group-wise protocol in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

Target normalization is computed within each workload group. Because every group is assigned wholly to one outer fold, no training label depends on a held-out row. Fold assignment is a function of the sorted set of workload-group keys and of seed~42 alone, so the platform-specific, zero-shot, and joint evaluations all use the identical 45-group partition.

Each placement is represented using only plan-level information and isolated profiles. For each instance, we compute twelve values: the assigned request rate in fps, three device-assignment indicators for CPU, GPU, and NPU, two model-type indicators for vision and generative models, four isolated-profile values for the assigned device, the load factor, and one interaction term. The isolated-profile values are inference latency in ms, model load time in ms, token generation rate in tok/s for generative models, and capacity defined as $1000/\mathrm{latency}$ in fps. The load factor is the request rate divided by capacity, and the interaction term is the product of request rate and isolated latency.

Aggregating each of the twelve values over all instances of a placement with sum, mean, and maximum yields 36 features, and the instance count $N$ completes the 37-dimensional representation. The feature list is identical on both platforms, and platform differences appear only in feature values. Execution settings and the software stack appear in Appendix~G.


\subsection*{G. Execution settings and software stack}
Both platforms share the host, schedules, and measurement procedure. They differ only in the accelerator execution path. Each window uses a 20\,s warm-up followed by a 180\,s measurement on a $1280{\times}720$ input video, with batch size~1 throughout and placement order randomized using seed~20260715. Vision models run at their native input sizes, with one worker process per instance. YOLO11 uses a $640{\times}640$ letterboxed input, and ResNet50 and MobileNet-v2 use $224{\times}224$ inputs.

On the GPU platform, vision models execute in FP32 through ONNX Runtime~1.20.1 with the CUDA execution provider, while generative models execute in FP16 through PyTorch~2.12.

On the NPU platform, vision models execute as INT8-quantized compiled binaries on eight cores via the vendor model zoo. Generative models use vendor 8-bit-weight checkpoints under \texttt{mobilint-qb-runtime}~1.2.0. LLaMA runs on one core, and the VLM uses one text core and four vision cores.

On both platforms, CPU-placed vision models execute in FP32 through the same ONNX Runtime~1.20.1 build with the CPU execution provider, and the CPU-placed LLM runs in FP32 through PyTorch~2.12 with the same greedy decoding settings. Thread counts are left at the runtime defaults. Each instance runs in its own process with its own ONNX Runtime intra-op thread pool, so windows with several CPU-placed instances oversubscribe the 24 physical cores. In vision workers the PyTorch thread pool, which is used only for post-processing, is limited to one thread. The intra-op thread count affects CPU inference latency and therefore the capacity and load-factor features of every CPU-placed instance. Both platforms use the same Python environment and the same model files for CPU execution, and across all 540 paired windows the CPU-assigned instance sets and the CPU-side feature contributions are identical. The thread configuration is therefore a shared property of the two platforms rather than a source of asymmetry between them.

Generation uses greedy decoding and is capped at 64 new tokens for the LLM and 32 new tokens for the VLM. The LLM cycles through a fixed prompt set across requests, and the VLM uses a fixed prompt with one video frame. Collection-time NVIDIA driver, CUDA/cuDNN build, and vendor compiler versions were not recorded and are omitted rather than reconstructed.

\textbf{Divergence margins.} For each of the 11 disagreeing groups, we compute one margin per platform between its best measured score and the score attained there by the other platform's best placement. The smallest of the 22 margins is $2.6\times10^{-3}$ in normalized score---six orders of magnitude above the $10^{-9}$ tie tolerance---and the median is $0.155$. At $\alpha{=}0.3$ and $\beta{=}1.0$ every workload group's best-placement set is a singleton, so the tie rule does not affect any reported margin.

\textbf{Score-equivalence bands.} Only two of the 22 margins fall below $0.01$, and they lie in two different groups. They are the GPU side of S8 at rate 2.0 ($2.6\times10^{-3}$) and the NPU side of base3 at rate 3.0 ($6.8\times10^{-3}$). Under a coarser band of $0.01$ that treats a group as agreeing when \emph{either} of its two margins falls within the band, the divergence count would be 9 of 45. That criterion is permissive: base3 at rate 3.0 would count as agreeing even though its GPU-side margin is $0.865$. Requiring \emph{both} margins to fall within the band leaves the count unchanged at 11 of 45. That holds for every band we examined up to $0.05$, since no group has both of its margins inside the band. Because each placement was measured once, these margins do not quantify run-to-run uncertainty. Repeated measurements would be needed to establish a noise-aware equivalence threshold.

\textbf{Nature of the disagreements.} In all 11 disagreeing groups the two platforms place the same generative instances and differ only in which vision model runs on the CPU. In seven of them the NPU offloads ResNet50 to the CPU while the GPU keeps every instance on the accelerator. This is consistent with the capacity estimates in Appendix~D. The VLM consumes $0.13$--$0.40$ of the estimated NPU capacity against $0.02$--$0.07$ on the GPU, so the larger estimated VLM load on the NPU leaves less estimated accelerator capacity for vision work, which is consistent with a different CPU-offload trade-off across the two platforms.

\textbf{Selected-placement quality.} Complementing Top-1, the ratio between the selected placement's measured score and the best measured score in its group averages $0.996$/$0.993$ (GPU/NPU) for direct regression, with a worst case of $0.921$/$0.910$ and no group below $0.9$. The decomposed baseline averages $0.980$/$0.951$ but has more low-ratio cases: its worst is $0.249$/$0.455$, and one GPU group and six NPU groups fall below $0.9$. Greedy averages $0.734$/$0.793$ over measured outputs under speedup ordering, and $0.538$/$0.581$ when unmeasured outputs count as zero. Under the normalized score this ratio can be negative.
