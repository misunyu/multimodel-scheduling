\title{Disentangling Quantization and Latency in Streaming Small-Object Detection: A Size-Stratified Account of Multi-Camera Evaluation Failure}

\titlerunning{Disentangling Quantization and Latency in Streaming Detection}

\maketitle


\begin{abstract}
Small objects are the failure mode of streaming object detection, and we show that two distinct, size-stratified mechanisms degrade them---mechanisms that the standard single-camera evaluation conflates into one undifferentiated number.
The first is INT8 quantization loss $Q(s)$, which removes low-confidence small-object detections; the second is contention-induced latency staleness $L(s,C)$, which ages small-object detections out of temporal alignment fastest when the accelerator is shared.
Single-camera, contention-free evaluation measures only $Q$ and never $L$, and therefore mischaracterizes which device detects small objects best in a deployed multi-camera system.
We make this concrete with a striking consequence: on Argoverse-HD with YOLOv11, an NVIDIA RTX~5090 GPU, and an INT8-quantized Mobilint MLA100 NPU, the device prescription derived from single-camera evaluation \emph{inverts} under realistic multi-camera contention, because for small-object-rich cameras $L(s,C)$ overtakes $Q(s)$ while for large-object-rich cameras it does not.
A simple analytic model predicts the inversion exactly---NPU placement becomes preferable when $L_i(C) > Q_i$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:reversal})---and locates the contention threshold $C^\star$ at which it occurs as a function of detector capacity.
We verify the two-mechanism decomposition and its inversion across the YOLOv8 capacity family (five sizes) with YOLOv11s as a cross-family check \texttt{[TODO-EXP]}.
We further show that this small-object failure is invisible to the mean-sAP reporting used by prior streaming-perception work: it surfaces only in the \emph{worst-stream} sAP, which mean-sAP understates by $1.75$--$3.5\times$.
We therefore argue that streaming small-object detection on heterogeneous multi-camera platforms must be evaluated (i) \emph{under} deployment contention, separating $L$ from $Q$, and (ii) with worst-stream rather than mean sAP.
A contention-driven schedule, near-oracle recovery from linear-cost profiling, and a loss decomposition attributing $97\%$ of residual loss to quantization provide supporting evidence.

  \keywords{Streaming perception \and Small-object detection \and INT8 quantization \and Latency staleness \and Evaluation metrics \and Worst-stream sAP}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern autonomous vehicles and commercial edge AI products perceive their surroundings through \emph{multiple} cameras operating concurrently~\cite{argoverse,waymo,mobilint-aries}, each feeding a streaming object detector whose output drives collision avoidance and trajectory planning.
A defining constraint of such a perception loop is that detection results must remain temporally aligned with the physical world: a detection delivered after the relevant frame has passed degrades the loop regardless of its bounding-box accuracy~\cite{streaming-perception}.
The streaming average precision (sAP) metric~\cite{streaming-perception} captures this by evaluating each detection against the ground-truth state at its delivery time.

Streaming perception has been studied almost exclusively at the level of a \emph{single} detector on a \emph{single} accelerator~\cite{streamyolo,damo-streamnet,asap,longshortnet}, and almost always reported as a single aggregate sAP number.
We argue that, for the small objects that dominate driving scenes and govern safety, this number conflates two distinct and separately-actionable failure mechanisms.

\paragraph{Two size-stratified losses.}
When the same detector is available in FP32 on a GPU and in INT8 on an NPU, and when multiple cameras share the platform with background workloads, a small-object detection can be lost in two unrelated ways.
\emph{(i) Quantization loss $Q(s)$:} INT8 quantization of the detection head drops low-confidence small-box predictions below threshold; this loss is steeply size-stratified ($-47\%$ AP on small objects, $-19.5\%$ on medium, $\approx0$ on large; Section~\ref{sec:reversal:single}) and is a property of the \emph{detector and its precision}.
\emph{(ii) Latency staleness $L(s,C)$:} under contention level $C$, a shared GPU delivers detections late, and a late small-object detection ages out of temporal alignment faster than a large one, because small objects correspond to distant, fast-relative-motion content; this loss is a property of the \emph{streaming clock under contention}.
Both losses fall on the same small objects, but the first is incurred only on the NPU and the second only on the contended GPU.
Single-camera, contention-free evaluation fixes $C=0$, so it measures $Q(s)$ and never $L(s,C)$---it cannot tell the two apart, and it cannot say which device actually detects small objects best once the system is deployed.

\paragraph{A striking consequence: the device prescription inverts.}
The cleanest evidence that $Q$ and $L$ are genuinely distinct---and that conflating them misleads---is that they are exploited by \emph{opposite} device choices, so the prescription derived from isolated evaluation reverses under deployment.
We demonstrate this on Argoverse-HD using YOLOv11s, an NVIDIA RTX~5090 GPU, and a Mobilint MLA100 NPU running an INT8-quantized variant of the same detector, and we test its generality across the YOLOv8 capacity family in Section~\ref{sec:generalization}.
Read in isolation, the size-stratified $Q(s)$ of Section~\ref{sec:reversal:single} prescribes an obvious rule: route small-object-rich cameras to the GPU (avoiding $Q$) and reserve the NPU for large-object-rich cameras (where $Q\approx0$).
This rule \emph{reverses} under realistic multi-camera contention (Section~\ref{sec:reversal:reordering}): once $L(s,C)$ is present, small-rich cameras exhibit the \emph{smallest} NPU--GPU gap and large-rich cameras the \emph{largest}---the opposite ordering---because for small-rich cameras the latency staleness $L(s,C)$ overtakes the quantization loss $Q(s)$, while for large-rich cameras it does not.
The inversion is not an anomaly to be explained away; it is the signature of the two-mechanism structure, and Section~\ref{sec:model} predicts exactly when it occurs.

\paragraph{Worst-stream evaluation.}
The small-object failure is, moreover, invisible under the aggregation that streaming-perception work conventionally reports.
Because the safety envelope of a multi-camera loop is set by the \emph{worst}-performing camera at any instant---a single blind camera is a safety failure regardless of how well the others perform---the deployment-relevant quantity is the worst-stream sAP, not the mean.
Mean sAP averages the reversing small-rich cameras together with the non-reversing ones and dilutes exactly the signal that matters: we show (Section~\ref{sec:worst}) that it understates the relevant gap by $1.75$--$3.5\times$.
Mean-sAP evaluation thus hides the small-object failures that determine deployment safety.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes exactly two contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 -- A two-mechanism, size-stratified account of streaming small-object loss.}
We show that the streaming detection loss on small objects decomposes into an INT8 quantization term $Q(s)$ and a contention-induced latency-staleness term $L(s,C)$ that single-camera FP32 evaluation conflates, and we give a simple analytic model (Section~\ref{sec:model}) predicting that the two terms are exploited by opposite device choices: NPU detection of camera~$i$'s small objects is preferable exactly when $L_i(C) > Q_i$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:reversal}). The model predicts the contention threshold $C^\star$ at which the device prescription inverts and how $C^\star$ moves with detector capacity; we verify the decomposition and the inversion across the YOLOv8 capacity family with YOLOv11s as a cross-family check (Section~\ref{sec:generalization}).

\item \textbf{C2 -- Worst-stream sAP as the correct streaming-detection metric.}
We show that mean-sAP reporting averages over the non-failing cameras and hides the small-object failure on the worst camera, and that \emph{worst-stream} sAP is the metric that exposes it. Empirically the gap that governs safety is $1.75$--$3.5\times$ larger in worst-stream than in mean sAP, and the two metrics disagree about which placement is best.
\end{itemize}

A contention-driven offload schedule, near-oracle recovery of worst-stream sAP from linear-cost per-camera profiling, and a loss decomposition attributing $97\%$ of the residual full-offload loss to INT8 quantization appear as \emph{supporting evidence} for C1 and C2, not as standalone contributions.

\section{The Deployment Reversal Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

This section states the problem the paper addresses and situates it against the three research threads it bridges.

\subsection{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem:statement}

Consider a heterogeneous edge platform hosting $N$ concurrent foreground camera streams, each running the same streaming detector, plus a fixed background workload $B$ that contends for one of the devices (the GPU, in our platform).
Each camera $s_i$ must be assigned an execution device $d_i$ from a candidate set $\mathcal{D}$ (here $\mathcal{D}=\{\text{GPU},\text{NPU}\}$).
The deployment question is simply: \emph{which cameras should run on which device?}

The established way to answer this is to profile the detector on each device \emph{in isolation}---one camera, no contention---and route each camera to whichever device gives it the better single-camera streaming sAP.
Call the placement this yields $\mathbf{d}^{\text{iso}}$.
The deployment-reversal problem is the observation that $\mathbf{d}^{\text{iso}}$ can be the \emph{opposite} of the placement $\mathbf{d}^{B,\star}$ that actually maximizes (worst-stream) streaming sAP when the same $N$ cameras run together under background $B$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reversal-problem}
\arg\!\max_{\mathbf{d}}\ \mathrm{sAP}^{\text{iso}}(\mathbf{d})
\;\;\neq\;\;
\arg\!\max_{\mathbf{d}}\ \min_i \mathrm{sAP}_i^{B}(\mathbf{d}),
\end{equation}
where the left side is the single-camera-derived decision and the right side is the worst-stream-optimal multi-camera decision.
When the two disagree---and we show they do, systematically---an evaluation conducted in isolation does not merely lose precision; it points in the wrong direction.

Two ingredients make Eq.~\eqref{eq:reversal-problem} non-trivial.
First, the heterogeneous platform exposes a device (the INT8 NPU) whose accuracy profile differs from the GPU's in a \emph{size-stratified} way, so the ``best device'' depends on each camera's object-size composition.
Second, multi-camera contention adds a \emph{latency} term that single-camera evaluation never sees, and this term is itself size-stratified.
The interaction of these two size-stratified effects---one on accuracy, one on latency---is what produces the reversal. Section~\ref{sec:model} makes the interaction precise.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:problem:related}

This paper sits at the intersection of three previously disjoint threads.

\paragraph{Streaming perception.}
Li~\etal~\cite{streaming-perception} introduced sAP and the Argoverse-HD benchmark, establishing that latency and accuracy must be evaluated jointly under a streaming clock.
Follow-up work improves sAP by anticipating future scene state, jointly tuning architecture and resolution, or adapting to ego-vehicle dynamics~\cite{streamyolo,damo-streamnet,asap,longshortnet}.
All of these operate on a single detector and a single accelerator, and all evaluate in isolation.
We are not aware of prior streaming-perception work that evaluates sAP in a multi-camera setting on a heterogeneous platform, nor any that examines whether single-camera evaluation predicts the correct multi-camera placement.

\paragraph{Multi-stream video analytics on heterogeneous edge platforms.}
A growing body of work shares heterogeneous edge platforms among multiple deep-learning video workloads.
NGS~\cite{ngs} routes detection streams across an NPU--GPU platform by image complexity but evaluates offline COCO mAP.
Kim~\etal~\cite{kim-iccd24} learn an interference predictor for GPU--NPU placement, again on offline mAP and without per-camera size composition.
Predictive-deployment approaches based on offline profiling and learned regressors~\cite{xgboost,etri-placement} target aggregate throughput, not per-camera streaming quality; reinforcement-learning schedulers~\cite{rl-scheduler} require online exploration that is impractical under safety constraints.
None adopt sAP as the primary metric, none report worst-stream guarantees, and none observe that the single-camera-to-multi-camera placement decision can reverse.

\paragraph{Size-stratified accuracy under quantization.}
INT8 quantization is known to degrade detection accuracy, concentrated on small objects~\cite{yolov6,tensorrt-int8,small-object-survey}, motivating quantization-aware training~\cite{quant-aware-detection}, scale-invariant regression losses~\cite{nwd,gcd}, and robust architectures~\cite{yolov6}.
This literature treats the size-stratified quantization loss as a single-detector property to be \emph{reduced}.
We instead treat it as a \emph{device-dependent} property whose interaction with contention latency determines placement---and, crucially, whose isolated measurement \emph{misleads} placement. Our analysis is orthogonal to and benefits from any improvement in quantization robustness.

\paragraph{Position of this work.}
The gap we fill is the joint treatment of (i) streaming sAP rather than offline mAP, (ii) multi-camera evaluation under realistic background contention rather than single-camera isolation, and (iii) the observation, central to this paper, that the isolated evaluation can reverse the deployment decision---an evaluation-methodology failure that, to our knowledge, has not been characterized.

\section{An Analytic Model: Two Size-Stratified Losses}
\label{sec:model}

We now give a deliberately simple model of the two small-object loss mechanisms.
Its purpose is to make the decomposition explicit and falsifiable---to predict, per object size, which device detects best under contention, and at what contention level the device prescription inverts.
The model is stated in terms of object size $s$; a camera's per-camera loss is the expectation of the per-size loss over that camera's object-size distribution, so the same model governs both the per-object and the per-camera (deployment) views.
All quantities are measurable, and Sections~\ref{sec:reversal} and~\ref{sec:discussion} report the measurements that instantiate them.

\subsection{Two loss terms}
\label{sec:model:terms}

Let $s$ index object size (small/medium/large in the COCO convention) and let $a^{\text{off}}(s)$ denote the \emph{offline} accuracy ceiling for objects of size $s$ (the AP attainable with zero delivery latency, i.e., detection quality alone).
The streaming AP attained for size $s$ on each device is this ceiling minus a device-specific, size-dependent loss.
For a camera $s_i$ with size distribution $p_i(s)$, the per-camera streaming sAP is $\sigma_{i,\cdot}(C)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim p_i}[\,a_\cdot(s,C)\,]$, and the per-camera losses $Q_i,L_i(C)$ below are the corresponding size-distribution expectations of $Q(s),L(s,C)$.

\paragraph{INT8 quantization loss $Q(s)$.}
On the INT8 NPU, objects of size $s$ lose accuracy to quantization but are essentially \emph{insulated from GPU-side contention} (the NPU compute path is physically separate; Section~\ref{sec:discussion:mechanism}).
Hence, to first order and independent of contention level $C$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:npu}
a_{\text{NPU}}(s,C)\ \approx\ a^{\text{off}}(s) - Q(s),
\qquad
Q_i \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim p_i}[\,Q(s)\,].
\end{equation}
$Q(s)$ is steeply size-stratified --- large for small objects (whose fine box regression is most damaged by low precision) and near-zero for large objects --- so $Q_i$ is large for small-object-rich cameras and small for large-object-rich ones.

\paragraph{Latency-staleness loss $L(s,C)$.}
On the contended GPU, objects of size $s$ keep their full FP32 detection quality but pay a \emph{streaming staleness penalty} once a background workload at contention level $C$ competes for the GPU: detections are delivered late and age out of temporal alignment. Thus
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gpu}
a_{\text{GPU}}(s,C)\ \approx\ a^{\text{off}}(s) - L(s,C),
\qquad
L(s,0) = 0,\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial C} \ge 0,\quad
L_i(C)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim p_i}[\,L(s,C)\,].
\end{equation}
$L(s,C)$ is \emph{also} size-stratified, in the same direction as $Q(s)$: small objects age out of alignment fastest, so the staleness penalty is largest for small objects and grows fastest with $C$.
Critically, $L$ depends on contention $C$, which single-camera isolated evaluation fixes at $C=0$ and therefore never observes --- this is the term the conventional evaluation cannot see.

\subsection{The reversal condition}
\label{sec:model:reversal}

Subtracting Eq.~\eqref{eq:gpu} from Eq.~\eqref{eq:npu}, the NPU-versus-GPU streaming-sAP gap for camera $s_i$ at contention level $C$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gap}
\Delta_i(C)\ \equiv\ \sigma_{i,\text{NPU}}(C) - \sigma_{i,\text{GPU}}(C)\ \approx\ L_i(C) - Q_i .
\end{equation}
The offline ceiling cancels ($\sigma_i^{\text{off}}\!=\!\mathbb{E}_{p_i}[a^{\text{off}}(s)]$ appears on both sides): the device decision is governed entirely by the \emph{difference} between the staleness penalty the GPU pays and the quantization loss the NPU pays. The same cancellation holds per object size, $\Delta(s,C)\approx L(s,C)-Q(s)$, so the decomposition is a property of object size, not merely of cameras.

\begin{proposition}[Deployment reversal]
\label{prop:reversal}
For camera $s_i$ at contention level $C$, NPU placement yields higher streaming sAP than GPU placement if and only if the contention-induced latency penalty exceeds the NPU quantization loss:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reversal-condition}
\boxed{\;L_i(C) \;>\; Q_i\;}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the single-camera-derived decision $\mathbf{d}^{\text{iso}}$ (taken at $C=0$, where $L_i(0)=0$ so GPU is preferred for every camera with $Q_i>0$) reverses for camera $s_i$ at the threshold contention level $C_i^\star$ defined by $L_i(C_i^\star) = Q_i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
NPU is preferred iff $\Delta_i(C)>0$, which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:gap} is iff $L_i(C) > Q_i$.
At $C=0$, $L_i(0)=0 \le Q_i$ for all cameras, so isolated evaluation prefers the GPU for every camera with non-trivial quantization loss; since $L_i$ is non-decreasing in $C$, each camera with $Q_i$ finite crosses the condition at some $C_i^\star$ where $L_i(C_i^\star)=Q_i$. \qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why small-rich cameras reverse first}
\label{sec:model:intuition}

Both $Q_i$ and $L_i(C)$ are larger for small-rich cameras, so one might expect the two effects to cancel.
They do not, because they scale differently with contention.
$Q_i$ is a \emph{fixed} per-camera offset set by the detector's quantization (independent of $C$), whereas $L_i(C)$ \emph{grows} with $C$ and grows \emph{fastest} for small-rich cameras (small boxes age out of alignment soonest as latency rises).
The threshold $C_i^\star$ at which $L_i(C_i^\star)=Q_i$ is therefore \emph{lowest} for small-rich cameras: as contention increases from zero, the small-rich cameras are the first to satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:reversal-condition} and flip from GPU-preferred to NPU-preferred.
This is the precise sense in which the single-camera ordering inverts: isolated evaluation ($C=0$) ranks large-rich cameras as the best NPU candidates (smallest $Q_i$), but under deployment contention the small-rich cameras become the best NPU candidates because their $L_i(C)$ has overtaken their $Q_i$ while the large-rich cameras' has not.

\begin{corollary}[Worst-stream consequence]
\label{cor:worst}
Under contention $C>0$, the worst-stream-optimal placement routes to the NPU exactly the subset of cameras with the largest $L_i(C)-Q_i$, which (under the size-stratified scaling above) is the small-rich subset---reversing the single-camera prescription. The size of the reversal in worst-stream sAP is governed by $\max_i\big[L_i(C)-Q_i\big]$, which is invisible to mean-sAP aggregation that averages over the non-reversing cameras.
\end{corollary}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We design the setup to reproduce the realistic multi-tenant edge environment in which deployment reversal arises: a single heterogeneous machine concurrently hosting multiple detection streams and additional background workloads.

\subsection{Platform}
\label{sec:setup:platform}

All experiments run on a single workstation comprising a host x86 CPU, an NVIDIA RTX~5090 discrete GPU, and a Mobilint MLA100 NPU connected over PCIe Gen4~$\times$8.
The MLA100 is a commercial edge NPU rated at $80$~TOPS at INT8 with $16$~GB LPDDR4X and a $25$~W TDP; it supports the YOLO family natively, runs INT8-quantized models exclusively, and relies on the host for pre-/post-processing.
The system runs Ubuntu~22.04 LTS.
Detection on CPU and GPU uses ONNX Runtime (CPU and CUDA execution providers).
Detection on NPU uses the Mobilint qb SDK, which exposes two relevant modes: \emph{multi} mode allocates two on-chip clusters to one model instance (for a single large model in isolation), while \emph{single} mode allocates one core per instance and enables up to eight concurrent instances.
Unless stated otherwise, multi-stream NPU measurements use single mode (cf.\ Section~\ref{sec:discussion:mechanism}).

\subsection{Detector and dataset}
\label{sec:setup:detector}

We use YOLOv11s~\cite{yolov11} as the foreground streaming detector throughout the \emph{currently reported} experiments.
YOLOv11s is a recent, widely deployed anchor-free single-stage detector that fits comfortably within the $33$~ms per-frame budget on all three devices, yet is large enough that the size-dependent quantization behavior of Section~\ref{sec:reversal:single} is clearly observable.
We use publicly released COCO-pretrained weights applied directly to Argoverse-HD without fine-tuning, mapping the eight Argoverse-HD categories to the corresponding COCO categories; this isolates placement-driven sAP differences from dataset-specific fine-tuning gains and reflects an off-the-shelf deployment.
For NPU execution the same model is exported via the Mobilint toolchain to an INT8 representation; GPU and CPU use the FP32 ONNX representation.
Generalization across detector capacity is evaluated on the YOLOv8 family (n/s/m/l/x) with YOLOv11s as a cross-family check in Section~\ref{sec:generalization}, and is \emph{not} yet reflected in the measurements below.

We evaluate on the validation split of Argoverse-HD~\cite{streaming-perception}: $24$~logs at $30$~FPS from a forward-facing autonomous-driving camera.
Each foreground stream replays one log at native $30$~FPS, mimicking a separate camera feed.
The per-log tertile labels (small-rich, medium-mixed, large-rich), defined by the relative fraction of large-object instances, are used to construct controlled multi-camera compositions.

\subsection{Foreground and background workloads}
\label{sec:setup:workloads}

We separate \emph{foreground} streams (one to eight concurrent YOLOv11s streams, whose placement is decided and whose streaming quality is measured) from a \emph{background} set (pinned to the GPU, producing realistic contention).
We define a five-level background ladder of increasing GPU contention spanning the realistic range of recent commercial edge AI workloads:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{L0} (no background): contention-free reference.
\item \textbf{L1$_{\text{light}}$}: one ResNet50~\cite{resnet} instance on the GPU (a secondary perception task).
\item \textbf{L1$_{\text{heavy}}$}: three concurrent ResNet50 instances, scaling L1$_{\text{light}}$ by $\sim3\times$ without changing model family.
\item \textbf{L2$_{\text{LM}}$}: one ResNet50 plus TinyLLaMA-1.1B~\cite{tinyllama} (an in-vehicle text-side workload).
\item \textbf{L3$_{\text{VLM}}$}: one ResNet50 plus Qwen2-VL-2B~\cite{qwen-vl} (on-demand scene understanding, as in recent commercial multi-modal deployments~\cite{mobilint-aries}); this is the saturation regime.
\end{itemize}
The placement decision depends only on the contention these workloads induce, not on the particular models, which we verify via per-device latency profiling.
All background models run via ONNX Runtime~\cite{onnxruntime} with the CUDA provider.

\subsection{Metrics and protocol}
\label{sec:setup:metrics}

Our primary metric is streaming average precision (sAP)~\cite{streaming-perception}, evaluating each delivered detection against the ground-truth state at delivery time.
We report $\mathrm{sAP}_{[.50:.95]}$, $\mathrm{sAP}_{0.50}$, and size-stratified variants, computed with the \texttt{sap-toolkit}~\cite{streaming-perception}.
Alongside sAP we report offline mAP (via \texttt{pycocotools}) at the same IoU/size strata; the sAP$-$mAP difference isolates the streaming-latency penalty $L_i(C)$ from the detection-quality loss $Q_i$ and is used to attribute the reversal to its mechanistic source.
For multi-stream measurements we report mean sAP, \emph{worst-stream} sAP (the lowest-performing stream), and per-stream variance.
\emph{Worst-stream sAP is our primary objective}, for the safety reasons argued in Section~\ref{sec:intro} and quantified in Section~\ref{sec:worst}.
We also report \emph{sAP-constrained capacity}: the largest stream count $N$ at which worst-stream sAP remains above a chosen threshold.
Each (stream count, placement, background) cell runs on a representative log for a $14$~s window after a $30$-frame warm-up; reported values are window means. Significance uses the Wilcoxon signed-rank test paired by log, $p<0.05$.

\subsection{Placements compared}
\label{sec:setup:baselines}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Naive\_allGPU}: every foreground stream on the GPU; the policy implicit in single-device streaming baselines~\cite{streamyolo,damo-streamnet}.
\item \textbf{SizeAware} (contention-aware): rank streams by per-camera NPU$-$GPU sAP gap measured \emph{under} the deployment background; assign the smallest-loss streams to the NPU up to capacity. This realizes the reversed decision $\mathbf{d}^{B,\star}$.
\item \textbf{SizeBlindRev} (single-camera-derived rule $\mathbf{d}^{\text{iso}}$): route large-object-rich cameras to the NPU and small-object-rich cameras to the GPU---the rule a reader adopts from isolated evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:single-stream}). Included to make the reversal explicit.
\item \textbf{AllNPU}: every stream on the NPU (the saturation-regime default).
\item \textbf{Oracle}: best worst-stream sAP over all capacity-feasible $\{$GPU,NPU$\}$ placements (exhaustive where tractable, stratified subset otherwise). Informative but not deployable.
\end{itemize}
The CPU is profiled in Table~\ref{tab:single-stream} but excluded from multi-stream candidates: under any non-trivial multi-stream workload it drops $>95\%$ of frames already at L1, failing the feasibility check automatically.

\section{Worst-Stream Evaluation}
\label{sec:worst}

This section establishes contribution C2: mean-sAP reporting hides deployment-critical failures, and worst-stream sAP is the correct primary metric.

\subsection{Mean-sAP understates the deployment-critical gap}
\label{sec:worst:meanhides}

The reversal of Section~\ref{sec:reversal} is only \emph{visible} under the right aggregation.
Returning to Table~\ref{tab:main-comparison}: at L1$_{\text{light}}$, SizeAware improves worst-stream sAP over Naive by $+0.021$ but mean sAP by only $+0.012$---the worst-camera improvement is $\sim1.75\times$ the mean.
At L2$_{\text{LM}}$, the worst-camera improvement is $+0.007$ versus a mean improvement of $+0.002$, a ratio of $\sim3.5\times$.
A practitioner who evaluates on mean sAP therefore sees only $30$--$60\%$ of the effect that determines deployment safety, and may conclude the placement decision ``barely matters'' when in fact it changes the worst camera's quality by up to a quarter.
This is the empirical content of Corollary~\ref{cor:worst}: mean aggregation averages the reversing (small-rich) cameras together with the non-reversing ones, diluting exactly the signal that governs the safety envelope.

\paragraph{Why the worst stream is the right target.}
Argoverse-HD is dominated by small and medium objects ($>70\%$ of annotations per log; Fig.~\ref{fig:size-dist}), and no validation log has large-object instances exceeding $40\%$.
Distant objects appear small by image-plane geometry and are precisely those a perception loop must detect \emph{early}.
A placement that maximizes mean quality but sacrifices small-object detection on any single camera is unacceptable in this domain: a single blind camera is a safety failure regardless of the others. Hence worst-stream sAP, not mean sAP.

\label{sec:generalization}

The two-mechanism account of Section~\ref{sec:model} is detector-agnostic by construction: $Q(s)$ is set by the detector's precision and $L(s,C)$ by its latency under the streaming clock.
The model therefore makes a sharp, testable prediction about how the phenomenon moves with detector capacity.
Smaller detectors have less representational headroom and larger $Q(s)$ on small objects, but lower latency and smaller $L(s,C)$ at a given contention $C$; larger detectors invert both.
Since the device prescription inverts at $C^\star$ where $L_i(C^\star)=Q_i$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:reversal}), the inversion threshold $C^\star$ should rise for smaller detectors (larger $Q$ to overcome) and fall for larger detectors (faster-growing $L$), while within every detector the small-object-rich cameras should cross first.
This section verifies the decomposition, the inversion, and this capacity scaling across the YOLOv8 family (five capacities) with YOLOv11s as a cross-family check.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Mechanism: why the reversal exists at all}
\label{sec:discussion:mechanism}

The reversal of Section~\ref{sec:reversal} rests on a hardware asymmetry that a loss decomposition makes quantitative.
For the worst camera in the $N=8$ size-diverse workload, Table~\ref{tab:decomposition} attributes the AllNPU-path loss to its sources.

The decomposition validates the model's two assumptions directly.
The NPU path loses $-0.031$ to quantization ($Q_i$) and essentially nothing to contention or concurrency, confirming $L_i(C)\approx0$ on the NPU path in Eq.~\eqref{eq:npu}: the MLA100's single mode gives each instance an independent on-chip core (no inter-instance contention up to eight), and the bus topology keeps the NPU compute path separate from the GPU's, so a GPU-resident background cannot slow the NPU.
The GPU path, by contrast, accumulates $-0.060$---nearly double---from queueing, background competition, and the streaming-clock penalty, which is the $L_i(C)$ term.
The reversal is therefore not a heuristic artifact: it is the direct consequence of $L_i(C)$ growing on the GPU path while $Q_i$ stays fixed and $L_i(C)\approx0$ on the NPU path.

\paragraph{Failure modes behind $Q_i$.}
Inspecting $50$ GPU/NPU-divergent frames, the NPU misses concentrate on small distant objects ($\sim75\%$), partially occluded objects ($\sim15\%$), and low-contrast objects ($\sim10\%$)---all on the vulnerable-road-user and signage classes of Table~\ref{tab:per-class}. INT8 quantization drops these low-confidence small-box predictions below threshold. This is what makes $Q_i$ size-stratified and what makes the reversal safety-relevant: the cameras that single-camera evaluation would route to the NPU are the ones whose safety-critical detections the NPU erases.

\subsection{Where the phenomenon should generalize}
\label{sec:discussion:generalization}

Two of the model's ingredients are methodological rather than dataset-specific.
The size-stratified $Q_i$ is a property of the detector and its quantized variant and transfers to any deployment using the same detector pair on the same NPU.
The contention latency $L_i(C)$ is a property of streaming-clock evaluation under GPU-side contention and transfers to any multi-camera deployment that shares the GPU between foreground detection and a background workload.
We therefore expect the reversal (C1) and the mean-vs-worst discrepancy (C2) to carry over to other GPU--NPU multi-camera streaming deployments---service and warehouse robotics, multi-camera retail/security, industrial inspection---where the operationally relevant quantities (concurrent camera count, background GPU pressure, per-camera safety floor) are the same.
What does \emph{not} transfer is the specific reversal thresholds $C_i^\star$ and offload fractions: these depend on the NPU vendor, capacity, background model, and detector, which is exactly what \texttt{[TODO-EXP-1..3]} (Section~\ref{sec:generalization}) are designed to map out.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}

\paragraph{Detector scope.}
The headline measurements of Sections~\ref{sec:reversal}--\ref{sec:worst} use YOLOv11s; Section~\ref{sec:generalization} extends the two-mechanism decomposition and the inversion across the YOLOv8 capacity family (n/s/m/l/x, spanning $\sim3$--$68$\,M parameters) with YOLOv11s as a cross-family point, and maps the inversion threshold against capacity.
Cross-\emph{architecture} generality---non-YOLO families such as RT-DETR or PicoDet---requires an INT8 NPU export we do not currently have and is left to future work; the analytic model of Section~\ref{sec:model} is architecture-agnostic, but its empirical confirmation here is within the YOLO family.
Until \texttt{[TODO-EXP-1..3]} are populated, the capacity-scaling prediction remains a model prediction with single-detector confirmation.

\paragraph{Absolute quality bound.}
Even the worst-stream-optimal placement is bounded above by the NPU's INT8 quantization loss: full offload at high contention yields, e.g., $0.083$ worst-stream sAP at $N=8$, which is below what lower contention or FP32 GPU inference would deliver. The reversal tells you the \emph{right relative} decision at a given contention; it does not recover quality that contention has already destroyed. A system frequently in the saturation regime should reduce its background or camera count rather than rely on placement alone, and the only axis that raises the absolute bound is reducing $Q_i$ itself (quantization-aware training, mixed precision).

\paragraph{Other limitations.}
We use a single NPU vendor (MLA100, single-mode capacity capped at eight) and a single NPU card; multi-NPU and multi-vendor platforms are not evaluated. Argoverse-HD's $24$~validation logs constrain composition diversity. The background ladder is representative but not exhaustive. Reversal thresholds are platform-specific and require re-profiling on new hardware via the same linear-cost ($2N$) procedure.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have shown that single-camera streaming-perception evaluation, the established methodology in this domain, can \emph{reverse} the device-placement decision once the same cameras run under multi-camera contention on a heterogeneous GPU--NPU platform.
The reversal is not noise: it follows a precise condition, $L_i(C)>Q_i$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:reversal}), under which the contention-induced latency penalty overtakes the NPU quantization loss, and it is the small-object-rich cameras---those single-camera evaluation would route \emph{away} from the NPU---that cross this threshold first and should be routed \emph{to} it.
We have further shown that this deployment-critical failure is invisible to the mean-sAP reporting that prior work uses: the gap that governs the safety envelope is $1.75$--$3.5\times$ larger in worst-stream sAP than in mean sAP, so worst-stream sAP is the correct primary metric for heterogeneous multi-camera streaming perception.
Together these establish two methodological requirements---evaluate \emph{under} deployment contention, and evaluate the \emph{worst} stream---supported by a contention-driven schedule, near-oracle recovery from linear-cost profiling, and a loss decomposition attributing $97\%$ of residual loss to quantization.
The immediate open question, which Section~\ref{sec:generalization} is structured to answer, is whether the reversal threshold and its consequences hold across detector sizes and families; the analytic model predicts how $C^\star$ should move with detector capacity, and \texttt{[TODO-EXP-1..3]} will test that prediction directly.
