\titlerunning{Disentangling Quantization and Latency in Multi-Camera Streaming Detection}

\maketitle


\begin{abstract}
Streaming object detection underpins multi-camera perception on vehicles and edge devices, yet it is almost always evaluated as a single streaming-accuracy (sAP) number from one camera in isolation. We show this isolated evaluation recommends a device placement that points the wrong way in deployment. On a heterogeneous GPU--NPU platform, accuracy is lost in two opposite, size-dependent ways: INT8 quantization on the NPU hurts small objects most---though even large objects lose about a fifth of their accuracy---while contention latency on the shared GPU hurts large objects most, as their fast-moving boxes go stale when results arrive late. Seeing only the first loss, isolated evaluation sends large-object-rich cameras to the NPU and small-rich ones to the GPU. Under multi-camera contention this reverses: the small-rich cameras it keeps on the GPU are the ones that should move to the NPU. A simple model explains the flip, and a contention-aware placement that follows it gives the best worst-stream accuracy---beating the isolated rule and both uniform policies---shifting toward all-NPU as cameras are added. The failure is also invisible to the mean accuracy prior work reports, which hides the worst camera that governs safety. Streaming detection on heterogeneous platforms should therefore be evaluated under contention and reported by worst-stream accuracy.
\keywords{Streaming perception \and Small-object detection \and INT8 quantization \and Edge inference \and Evaluation metrics}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Autonomous vehicles and commercial edge products perceive their surroundings through \emph{several} cameras at once~\cite{argoverse,waymo,mobilint-aries}. Each camera feeds a streaming object detector whose output drives collision avoidance and planning, and a detection is useful only while it is still aligned with the world: a result delivered after the relevant frame has passed degrades the control loop no matter how accurate its boxes are~\cite{streaming-perception}. The streaming average precision (sAP) metric~\cite{streaming-perception} captures this by scoring each detection against the scene state at its \emph{delivery} time.

Streaming perception has been studied almost entirely for a \emph{single} detector on a \emph{single} accelerator, and reported as a single aggregate sAP number~\cite{streamyolo,damo-streamnet,asap,longshortnet}. Modern edge platforms, however, are heterogeneous: a GPU runs the detector in FP32, while an INT8 NPU runs a quantized copy at far lower power. With several cameras sharing such a platform, a natural question is which device should process each camera's stream---and the established way to answer it is an evaluation protocol: profile each detector on each device in isolation (one stream, no competing workload) and route each stream to its better device. We show that this protocol can point the \emph{wrong} way, because the isolated measurement cannot see a size-stratified loss that appears only under deployment contention.

\paragraph{Two opposite size-stratified losses.}
Detection quality is lost in two unrelated, size-dependent ways, on two different devices (Fig.~\ref{fig:reversal}). \emph{(i)~Quantization loss.} On the INT8 NPU, low-precision arithmetic drops the low-confidence predictions of small objects below threshold. This loss is steeply size-stratified---largest on small objects and smaller on large ones, though not zero even there ($-21\%$ on large; Sec.~\ref{sec:reversal:single})---and is a property of the \emph{detector and its precision}. \emph{(ii)~Latency staleness.} On the GPU, boxes keep their full accuracy but are delivered late once a background workload competes for the device. A late box ages out of alignment fastest for nearby, large-appearing objects, which traverse more pixels per frame, so this loss falls hardest on \emph{medium and large} objects and is a property of the \emph{streaming clock under contention}. The two losses run in \emph{opposite} size directions, and isolated evaluation, which fixes contention to zero, sees only the quantization loss and never the latency one. It therefore cannot tell the two apart, nor say which device a camera should use once deployed.

\paragraph{A striking consequence: the device choice reverses.}
Read in isolation, the quantization loss suggests an obvious rule: send small-object-rich cameras to the GPU and reserve the NPU for large-object-rich cameras, whose relative quantization loss is smallest. Under realistic multi-camera contention this rule points the wrong way. As streams pile onto the shared GPU, a camera's fixed quantization loss is overtaken by its growing latency loss, and the better device flips: the small-rich cameras the isolated rule keeps on the GPU are exactly the ones that should move to the NPU. A contention-aware placement that offloads them gives the best worst-stream accuracy of any deployable policy---better than leaving every stream on the GPU, better than the isolated rule, and better than blindly moving everything to the NPU---and the optimal offload fraction grows from none toward all streams as more cameras share the platform. Our model (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}) predicts when each camera reverses, and experiments on Argoverse-HD with YOLO11, an RTX~5090 GPU, and a Mobilint MLA100 NPU confirm it across capacity (Sec.~\ref{sec:generalization}).

\paragraph{The failure is hidden by mean sAP.}
The safety envelope of a multi-camera loop is set by its \emph{worst} camera at any instant---one blind camera is a failure regardless of the others---so the deployment-relevant quantity is the worst-stream sAP, not the mean. Averaging hides this: a placement can post a healthy mean while its worst camera sits near half that accuracy, and at eight concurrent cameras a GPU-bound policy keeps only two of eight streams alive where full NPU offload keeps all eight. Prior streaming work reports the mean, and so would miss exactly the worst-camera failure that governs safety.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes two contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1: A two-mechanism, size-stratified account of streaming loss, and the placement reversal it predicts.} Streaming loss separates into an INT8 quantization term concentrated on small objects and a contention-latency term concentrated on medium and large objects---two terms isolated evaluation conflates. A simple model (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}) makes NPU placement preferable exactly when the contention latency loss exceeds the quantization loss, which reverses the isolated rule under load: a contention-aware placement that offloads the reversing (small-rich) cameras gives the best deployable worst-stream sAP, and the optimal offload fraction grows toward all-NPU as cameras are added. We confirm the reversal across the YOLO11 family (Sec.~\ref{sec:generalization}).
\item \textbf{C2: Worst-stream sAP, not mean sAP, is the deployment-relevant metric.} Worst-case-over-subgroups reporting is standard in robustness and fairness~\cite{groupdro} and in multi-stream systems~\cite{biswift,videoedge}, but streaming-perception evaluation reports the mean and so averages away the worst camera. We show the distinction is decisive here: under a heavy co-tenant the worst camera can sit near half the mean accuracy, and the worst-stream view exposes a camera-survival collapse (two of eight vs.\ eight of eight streams) that the mean, computed over survivors, hides.
\end{itemize}
A contention-driven offload schedule whose optimal NPU fraction grows from zero to all streams as load rises, a contention-aware placement that recovers about $90\%$ of an exhaustive oracle from cheap per-camera profiling, and a loss decomposition showing the NPU path loses almost only to quantization while the GPU path loses more to contention latency, appear as \emph{supporting evidence}.

\section{The Reversal Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Problem statement.}
Consider a heterogeneous edge platform running several camera streams at once, each running its own copy of the same streaming detector on its own scene, alongside a fixed background workload that competes for one device (the GPU, on our platform). By ``multi-camera'' we mean this multi-tenant case---several \emph{independent} cameras whose detectors share one accelerator complex---not synchronized surround-view fusion; what couples the streams is contention for shared compute, not a joint scene model. Each camera must be sent to the GPU or the NPU. The established way to decide is to profile each device \emph{in isolation}---one stream, no contention---and route each camera to whichever device gave the better single-camera accuracy. The reversal problem is that this isolated choice can be the \emph{opposite} of the placement that actually maximizes the worst stream's accuracy when the same cameras run together under load. Two ingredients make this non-trivial: the INT8 NPU differs from the GPU in a \emph{size-dependent} way, so the best device depends on each camera's object-size mix; and multi-camera contention adds a \emph{latency} penalty, itself size-dependent, that isolated evaluation never sees. Their interaction produces the reversal, which Sec.~\ref{sec:model} makes precise.

\paragraph{Related work.}
\emph{Streaming perception.} Li~\etal~\cite{streaming-perception} introduced sAP and Argoverse-HD, showing latency and accuracy must be evaluated jointly under a streaming clock; later methods improve sAP by predicting future state, jointly tuning architecture and resolution, or adapting to ego-motion~\cite{streamyolo,damo-streamnet,asap,longshortnet}. All operate on a single detector and a single accelerator and report a single aggregate (mean) sAP. \emph{Heterogeneous multi-stream edge analytics.} A large body of work shares heterogeneous platforms among video streams: NGS~\cite{ngs} routes by image complexity, Kim~\etal~\cite{kim-iccd24} learn a GPU--NPU interference predictor, and predictive-placement~\cite{xgboost,etri-placement} and RL schedulers~\cite{rl-scheduler} target aggregate throughput. The systems community has long observed that profiling a workload \emph{in isolation} mispredicts its co-located behavior, motivating interference-aware provisioning~\cite{igniter}; that line, however, measures latency, throughput, and QoS---not detection accuracy---and never observes that the better \emph{device for accuracy} can reverse. \emph{Worst-case evaluation.} Reporting the worst subgroup rather than the mean is standard in robustness and fairness (worst-group accuracy, group-DRO~\cite{groupdro}), and multi-stream systems already pursue accuracy fairness across streams~\cite{biswift} or per-query minimum-accuracy constraints~\cite{videoedge}. We import this principle into \emph{streaming} sAP and show, to our knowledge for the first time, that it not only matters but \emph{reverses} the device decision a mean- or single-camera evaluation would make. \emph{Quantization and object size.} INT8 quantization is known to harm small-object detection~\cite{yolov6,tensorrt-int8,small-object-survey}, motivating quantization-aware training~\cite{quant-aware-detection}, scale-invariant losses~\cite{nwd,gcd}, and size/speed-driven DNN selection for single-stream edge detection~\cite{tod}; this literature treats the size-stratified loss as a single-detector property to \emph{reduce}, whereas we treat it as a \emph{device-dependent} term whose interaction with contention latency decides placement and whose isolated measurement misleads. To our knowledge, the joint treatment of streaming sAP, multi-camera contention, worst-stream evaluation, and the resulting placement reversal has not been characterized.

\section{A Simple Model of the Two Losses}
\label{sec:model}

We give a deliberately simple model whose only job is to make the two losses explicit and the reversal falsifiable. Take one camera's stream and, for each object size (small, medium, large), ask which device detects it better.

\paragraph{Quantization loss---the NPU's penalty.}
Running the detector in INT8 on the NPU lowers its accuracy by a \emph{quantization loss} that is a fixed property of the detector and its precision. This loss is largest on small objects, whose fine boxes are most damaged by low precision, and smaller on large objects---though not zero even there (about a fifth of large-object accuracy; Sec.~\ref{sec:reversal:single}). Crucially, it does \emph{not} depend on how busy the GPU is: the NPU runs on its own compute path (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion:mechanism}), so a camera's quantization loss is the same whether it runs alone or alongside seven others.

\paragraph{Latency loss---the GPU's penalty.}
Running the detector in full precision on the GPU keeps every box accurate, but once other work competes for the GPU the boxes are delivered late and fall out of step with the moving scene---a \emph{latency loss}. It grows with contention---the combined pressure of the background workload \emph{and} the other camera streams---and falls hardest on medium and large objects, whose nearby boxes sweep across many pixels per frame; small, distant objects barely move between frames and tolerate the delay. A single camera under a light background sees almost no latency loss; the same camera in a busy multi-camera deployment sees a large one. This is exactly the loss isolated evaluation cannot see, because it fixes contention at zero.

\paragraph{The reversal condition.}
A camera's accuracy on either device is its latency-free ceiling minus one of these two losses, so the ceiling cancels and the device choice comes down to a single comparison: which loss is larger.

\begin{proposition}[Reversal condition]
\label{prop:reversal}
The NPU gives higher streaming accuracy than the GPU exactly when a camera's latency loss exceeds its quantization loss. In isolation there is no contention, so the latency loss is zero and the GPU wins for every camera; as contention rises the latency loss grows until it overtakes the fixed quantization loss, and at that point the camera's better device flips to the NPU.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Which cameras reverse first.}
Both losses are larger for small-object-rich cameras, but the quantization loss is a fixed offset while the latency loss grows with contention, so within a workload the small-rich cameras reverse first---not because small objects suffer the most staleness (they suffer the least) but because their quantization loss is smaller still. Across detector sizes the contention level at which the flip happens is remarkably stable: every detector reverses only under near-saturation load (Sec.~\ref{sec:generalization}, Fig.~\ref{fig:gen-cstar}), so what scales with capacity is the size of the quantization loss, not the crossover. What never changes is the reversal itself.

\paragraph{Consequence for placement.}
Under any real contention the placement that maximizes the worst stream sends to the NPU exactly those cameras whose latency loss has overtaken their quantization loss. As contention grows this set expands from none (leaving every stream on the GPU is best under light load) through partial offload to all cameras (moving everything to the NPU is best under heavy load)---the opposite of the isolated rule, which is frozen at zero contention. The accuracy the isolated rule forgoes lands on the worst camera, which is exactly what averaging over streams hides---motivating the worst-stream metric of Sec.~\ref{sec:worst}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Platform.}
All experiments run on one workstation with a host x86 CPU, an NVIDIA RTX~5090 GPU, and a Mobilint MLA100 NPU (PCIe Gen4~$\times$8). The MLA100, built on Mobilint's ARIES NPU, is rated up to $80$~TOPS INT8 within $25$~W, exposes eight on-chip cores, and runs INT8 models exclusively. % [VERIFY-HW] confirm on-board LPDDR4X capacity before camera-ready.
GPU/CPU detection uses ONNX Runtime (CUDA/CPU); NPU detection uses the Mobilint SDK, whose \emph{single} mode gives each instance one core and supports up to eight concurrent instances (used for all multi-stream NPU runs; Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion:mechanism}).

\paragraph{Detector and dataset.}
The main experiments use YOLOv11s~\cite{yolov11}, a recent anchor-free detector light enough for real-time streaming yet large enough that the size-dependent quantization behavior is clear. On our platform the GPU runs it well within the $33$~ms per-frame budget; the INT8 NPU runs near the budget but---crucially for what follows---is insensitive to GPU-side contention (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion:mechanism}). % [VERIFY-LAT] single-stream NPU infer time measured at ~36 ms in the current SDK state (vs ~10 ms previously); confirm before camera-ready. We use public COCO-pretrained weights on Argoverse-HD without fine-tuning (its eight categories mapped to COCO), reflecting an off-the-shelf deployment; the NPU model is exported to INT8 via the Mobilint toolchain, GPU/CPU use FP32 ONNX. Generalization across capacity uses YOLO11 s/m/l/x (n pending an NPU export; Sec.~\ref{sec:generalization}). We evaluate on the Argoverse-HD validation split~\cite{streaming-perception} ($24$ logs at $30$~FPS from the forward-facing ring camera, the only one with streaming-sAP labels). Since our object of study is contention among \emph{independent} streams, we emulate an $N$-camera workload by replaying $N$ distinct logs concurrently---distinct scenes and size mixes, the heterogeneity placement must handle---capturing shared-compute contention but not a physically synchronized rig (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations}). Per-log tertile labels (small-/medium-/large-rich, by large-object fraction) build controlled compositions.

\paragraph{Workloads.}
We separate \emph{foreground} streams (one to eight YOLOv11s streams, whose placement we decide and quality we measure) from a \emph{background} set pinned to the GPU. The background ladder spans five levels of GPU pressure: \textbf{L0} (none); \textbf{L1$_{\text{light}}$} (one ResNet50~\cite{resnet}); \textbf{L1$_{\text{heavy}}$} (three ResNet50); \textbf{L2$_{\text{LM}}$} (ResNet50 + TinyLLaMA-1.1B~\cite{tinyllama}); and \textbf{L3$_{\text{VLM}}$} (ResNet50 + Qwen2-VL-2B~\cite{qwen-vl}), the saturation regime~\cite{mobilint-aries}. All background models run via ONNX Runtime~\cite{onnxruntime} on CUDA. The placement decision depends only on the contention these workloads induce, which we verify by per-device latency profiling.

\paragraph{Metrics and placements.}
Our primary metric is streaming AP (sAP)~\cite{streaming-perception}, scoring each delivered detection against the ground truth at its delivery time; we report $\mathrm{sAP}_{[.50:.95]}$, $\mathrm{sAP}_{0.50}$, and size-stratified variants, alongside offline mAP whose difference from sAP separates the latency loss from the quantization loss. For multi-stream runs we report mean and \emph{worst-stream} sAP, the latter our primary objective; each cell runs on a representative log over a $14$~s window after a $30$-frame warm-up, with Wilcoxon signed-rank significance paired by log ($p<0.05$). We compare five placements: \textbf{All-GPU}; \textbf{Isolated} (the rule from isolated evaluation: large-rich~$\to$~NPU, small-rich~$\to$~GPU); \textbf{Contention-aware} (rank cameras by the per-camera NPU$-$GPU gap measured \emph{under} the deployment background, and offload the smallest-loss streams); \textbf{All-NPU}; and \textbf{Oracle} (best worst-stream sAP over all feasible placements, not deployable). The CPU is excluded from multi-stream candidates: it drops $>95\%$ of frames at L1.

\section{Evidence of the Reversal}
\label{sec:reversal}

This section establishes C1: isolated evaluation prescribes one placement, multi-camera contention prescribes the opposite, and Proposition~\ref{prop:reversal} predicts which cameras reverse.

\paragraph{Isolated evaluation says ``small to the GPU''.}
\label{sec:reversal:single}
Table~\ref{tab:single-stream} profiles each device on Argoverse-HD in isolation.
The NPU's INT8 quantization induces a sharp, size-stratified loss in \emph{relative} terms: $-56\%$ on small objects, $-35\%$ on medium, and $-21\%$ on large---steepest on small objects, matching prior reports that INT8 disproportionately harms small-object box regression~\cite{yolov6,tensorrt-int8,small-object-survey}, but, importantly, far from zero even on large objects. This is the quantization loss: largest for small-rich cameras, smaller but still positive for large-rich ones. Read in isolation, the table prescribes the obvious placement---route each stream to its smaller-relative-loss device, sending small-rich streams to the GPU and large-rich streams to the NPU---which we carry as the \textbf{Isolated} rule. A per-class breakdown (supplementary) confirms the gap tracks object \emph{size}, not class label: the largest classes (bus, truck) lose least or even gain, while the safety-critical small classes (pedestrian, traffic light, stop sign) lose $30$--$60\%$ of their AP. Isolated evaluation would route exactly the safety-critical classes onto the device that detects them worst, and---as Sec.~\ref{sec:reversal:decision} shows---would also load the contended GPU with the streams least able to tolerate it. Fig.~\ref{fig:failures} shows representative frames where the INT8 NPU drops exactly these objects while the FP32 GPU retains them.

\paragraph{Multi-camera contention reverses the prescription.}
\label{sec:reversal:reordering}
Streams do not run alone: background workloads compete for the GPU, raising per-camera GPU latency and adding the size-dependent latency loss of Sec.~\ref{sec:model}. Running each of eight representative logs as a single foreground stream under a light background (L1), Table~\ref{tab:partA} reports the per-camera NPU$-$GPU accuracy gap, ranked by NPU loss. Two things stand out. First, the ranking already inverts Table~\ref{tab:single-stream}'s prescription: small-rich streams show the \emph{smallest} NPU penalty (mean gap $-0.014$) and large-rich the largest (mean gap $-0.039$), so the streams isolated evaluation sends to the GPU are precisely the ones best suited to the NPU. Second, every gap is still negative for a \emph{single} foreground stream---one stream under a light background does not yet contend enough to cross over. The crossing appears once several streams share the GPU: with four concurrent cameras the added contention pushes the small-rich streams past the point where the NPU wins, which is exactly the regime Sec.~\ref{sec:reversal:decision} measures. Fig.~\ref{fig:reversal} (right) confirms the source: the streaming gap exceeds the offline gap precisely for small-rich streams, so their extra loss is latency, not quality.

\paragraph{The reversal changes the deployment decision.}
\label{sec:reversal:decision}
Table~\ref{tab:main-comparison} evaluates five placements on a size-diverse four-camera workload (two small-rich, two large-rich) under two backgrounds, averaged over three runs. The result is decisive and matches the model. With four streams competing for the GPU, the \textbf{Contention-aware} placement---which offloads the small-rich cameras the isolated rule would have kept on the GPU---gives the best deployable worst-stream sAP under \emph{both} backgrounds ($0.100$ at L1$_{\text{light}}$, $0.067$ at L2$_{\text{LM}}$), beating All-GPU ($0.082$, $0.063$), the \textbf{Isolated} rule ($0.084$, $0.055$), and uniform All-NPU ($0.084$, $0.064$); it also leads on mean sAP. It recovers about $90\%$ of the exhaustive oracle ($0.110$, $0.074$) from cheap per-camera profiling alone. The single-camera Isolated rule (large-rich~$\to$~NPU, small-rich~$\to$~GPU) is never best and is the worst placement under the heavy co-tenant, precisely because it does the reverse of what Proposition~\ref{prop:reversal} prescribes---paying the NPU's quantization cost on the large-rich streams while leaving the small-rich streams on the saturated GPU. The cameras the isolated rule routes \emph{away} from the NPU are exactly the ones contention routes \emph{to} it.

\section{Worst-Stream Evaluation}
\label{sec:worst}

This section establishes C2. Reporting the worst subgroup rather than the mean is a standard robustness and fairness principle~\cite{groupdro} that streaming-perception evaluation has not adopted; we show it is decisive here---mean sAP hides deployment-critical failures, so worst-stream sAP is the deployment-relevant primary metric.

\paragraph{Mean sAP understates the deployment-critical gap.}
\label{sec:worst:meanhides}
The deployment-critical quantity is not the average over cameras but the worst one, and the two can be far apart. In Table~\ref{tab:main-comparison} under the heavy co-tenant, the Isolated rule posts a healthy-looking mean sAP of $0.103$ while its worst camera sits at $0.055$---barely half---so a practitioner reading the mean would see a routine result where one camera is in fact near-blind. The same gap drives the placement choice: the contention-aware policy improves the worst camera by up to two streaming-AP points over the next-best deployable option while moving the mean only slightly, so an evaluation that optimizes the mean barely distinguishes placements that differ sharply in safety. Because Argoverse-HD is dominated by small and medium objects ($>70\%$ of annotations per log; no log exceeds $40\%$ large)---exactly the distant objects a perception loop must detect early---and one blind camera is a safety failure regardless of the others, the deployment-relevant quantity is the worst-stream sAP, not the mean.

\paragraph{Worst-stream capacity.}
\label{sec:worst:capacity}
The worst-stream view also changes how many cameras a platform can safely sustain. Table~\ref{tab:capacity} sweeps the camera count $N$ at L1$_{\text{light}}$ (the $N=8$ row is a three-run mean; smaller-$N$ rows are single-run and direction-consistent). The best worst-stream strategy shifts exactly as Proposition~\ref{prop:reversal} implies: All-GPU is best at small $N$, partial contention-aware offload at $N=4$--$5$, and full NPU offload at $N\!\ge\!6$, as rising concurrency pushes more cameras past their crossover. The choice is consequential: at a worst-stream threshold of $0.08$, All-GPU sustains $4$ cameras while All-NPU sustains all $8$. The gap is starkest in raw survival---at $N=8$ under All-GPU only two of eight streams keep non-trivial throughput, whereas all eight stay active under All-NPU---a collapse to a functional two-camera system that mean sAP, computed over survivors, would mask.

\paragraph{The metric survives on natural compositions.}
\label{sec:worst:natural}
To check that worst-stream evaluation is not an artifact of an adversarial mix, we also evaluate two natural Argoverse-HD compositions (supplementary). There the per-camera NPU losses cluster around a single hardest-camera quantization floor, so the worst-stream-optimal policy simplifies to near-uniform full NPU offload (All-NPU within $0.005$ of the oracle in every $N=8$ cell). The reversal lever flattens---a property of the composition, not the metric---but in every cell the worst-stream-optimal placement still differs from what a mean-sAP or single-camera evaluation would choose, and the deployable default (All-NPU) is again the \emph{opposite} of the single-camera ``large~$\to$~NPU'' rule.

\section{Generalization Across Detector Capacity}
\label{sec:generalization}

The two-mechanism account is detector-agnostic by construction: the quantization loss is set by the detector's precision and the latency loss by its streaming clock under contention. We verify it across the YOLO11 family (s/m/l/x; YOLO11n pending an NPU export), with the YOLOv8 family as a same-protocol cross-family check (supplementary). The capacity sweep returns one prediction confirmed and one surprise: the two losses keep their opposite size directions at every capacity (confirmed), but the contention at which the device choice reverses barely moves with detector size (surprise)---what scales is the magnitude of the quantization loss, not the crossover.

Table~\ref{tab:gen-decomp} reports the per-size quantization loss and single-stream latency loss (at the L2 background) across the family, as a fraction of each detector's GPU baseline. Two patterns hold at every capacity and mirror the YOLOv11s anchor: the quantization loss concentrates on small objects (steepest on small, but non-trivial---$-21\%$ to $-41\%$---even on large), whereas the latency loss does \emph{not} concentrate on small ($+11\%$ to $+28\%$, flat-to-larger toward medium and large). The two losses thus run in opposite size directions across the whole capacity range, exactly as the model requires.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Mechanism: why the reversal exists.}
\label{sec:discussion:mechanism}
The reversal rests on a hardware asymmetry that a loss decomposition makes quantitative. For the worst camera in the $N=8$ size-diverse workload, Table~\ref{tab:decomposition} attributes its All-NPU-path loss to sources.
The NPU path loses $-0.057$ to quantization and nothing further to contention: adding the GPU-side background ($+0.026$) and seven concurrent NPU instances ($+0.000$) does not degrade it---their contributions are non-negative---so the NPU path's net loss ($-0.034$) is essentially all quantization, confirming the latency loss is negligible on the NPU path. Single mode gives each instance an independent on-chip core, and the bus topology keeps the NPU compute path separate from the GPU's. The GPU path, by contrast, loses more ($-0.059$), almost entirely to queueing, background competition, and the streaming-clock penalty---the latency loss that grows with load. The reversal is therefore not a heuristic artifact but the direct consequence of the latency loss growing on the GPU path while the quantization loss stays fixed and the latency loss stays near zero on the NPU path. Inspecting $50$ GPU/NPU-divergent frames, the NPU misses concentrate on small distant, partially occluded, and low-contrast objects (Fig.~\ref{fig:failures})---the vulnerable-road-user and signage classes whose detections the NPU erases.

\paragraph{Scope and generalization.}
\label{sec:discussion:generalization}
Both mechanisms are methodological rather than dataset-specific---the quantization loss a property of the detector and its quantized variant, the latency loss a property of streaming-clock evaluation under shared-GPU contention---so we expect the reversal and the mean-vs-worst discrepancy to carry over to other GPU--NPU multi-camera deployments (service and warehouse robotics, retail and security, industrial inspection); only the specific crossover points and offload fractions, which depend on vendor, capacity, background, and detector, do not transfer.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}
The headline measurements use YOLOv11s, with the YOLO11 family for capacity scaling; cross-architecture generality (non-YOLO families) needs an INT8 NPU export we lack, though the model itself is architecture-agnostic. We also observed that the NPU's measured behavior depends on the runtime/SDK state: an earlier sweep showed near-zero large-object quantization loss, but a repeatability test reproduced a $\sim\!-0.10$ large-object gap in $8/8$ runs, establishing it as the current-SDK normal state, which we use throughout; the single-camera NPU inference time likewise differs between SDK states. The multi-stream YOLO11-family gains of Table~\ref{tab:gen-gain} were collected in the earlier state and are being re-measured for consistency (Sec.~\ref{sec:generalization}). Even the worst-stream-optimal placement is bounded by the NPU's INT8 loss---it gives the right \emph{relative} decision at a given contention, not recovery of quality contention has destroyed; raising the absolute bound requires reducing the quantization loss itself (quantization-aware training, mixed precision). Finally, we use one NPU vendor and card, and emulate multi-camera workloads by replaying distinct single-camera logs (Argoverse-HD annotates only the forward ring camera); validating on a synchronized multi-camera benchmark with streaming labels is future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We showed that single-camera streaming-perception evaluation can reverse the device-placement decision once the same cameras run under multi-camera contention on a heterogeneous GPU--NPU platform. The cause is two opposite, size-stratified losses---INT8 quantization, steepest on small objects but non-negligible everywhere, and contention latency, heaviest on medium and large objects---that isolated evaluation conflates because it sees only the first. The reversal follows a precise condition (Proposition~\ref{prop:reversal}): the better device flips once a camera's contention latency on the GPU exceeds its fixed quantization loss. In practice this makes a contention-aware placement---one that offloads the small-rich cameras the isolated rule keeps on the GPU---the best deployable choice, recovering about $90\%$ of an exhaustive oracle, while the optimal offload fraction grows toward all-NPU as cameras are added; the isolated single-camera rule is never best. This failure is invisible to the mean accuracy prior work reports, which can mask a worst camera sitting near half the average. Heterogeneous multi-camera streaming detection should therefore be evaluated \emph{under} deployment contention and reported by \emph{worst-stream} sAP. Across the YOLO11 family the reversal holds at every capacity, arriving at a near-constant contention level; extending it to non-YOLO detectors and synchronized multi-camera benchmarks is the natural next step.
