\title{Quantization, Latency, and Device Placement Reversal in Multi-Camera Streaming Detection}

\titlerunning{Disentangling Quantization and Latency in Multi-Camera Streaming Detection}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Streaming object detection on heterogeneous edge platforms is commonly evaluated by profiling a detector on a single camera running alone and reporting a single aggregate streaming-AP (sAP) score. We show that this isolated evaluation protocol can recommend the wrong device once the detector is deployed on a multi-camera platform whose GPU is shared with a co-resident workload.
%
In isolation, the FP32 GPU outperforms the INT8 NPU because quantization loss is concentrated on small objects and negligible on large ones, leading isolated profiling to prescribe an All-GPU deployment. Under GPU-saturating co-tenancy, however, contention-induced staleness causes GPU streams to collapse while the NPU remains largely unaffected on a separate compute path. As a result, the device decision reverses: moving all cameras to the NPU improves worst-stream sAP by $4$--$5\times$ over the All-GPU placement.
%
Using a controlled staleness lever that leaves detections bit-identical while changing delivery latency, we causally separate two underlying losses: a load-stable quantization loss concentrated on small objects and a contention-induced staleness loss concentrated on medium and large objects. A contention sweep further shows that the reversal emerges at moderate GPU contention rather than only after complete saturation.
%
Finally, we show that the failure is fundamentally a worst-camera effect. Mean sAP can substantially understate failure severity once some streams remain healthy while others collapse. We therefore argue that heterogeneous multi-camera streaming detection should be evaluated under deployment-relevant co-tenancy and reported using worst-stream, not only mean, sAP.

\keywords{Streaming perception \and Heterogeneous edge inference \and INT8 quantization \and GPU--NPU placement \and Evaluation metrics}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Autonomous vehicles and commercial edge products perceive their surroundings through \emph{several} cameras at once~\cite{argoverse,waymo,mobilint-aries}. Each camera feeds a streaming object detector whose output drives planning, and a detection is useful only while it is still aligned with the world: a result delivered after the relevant frame has passed degrades the control loop no matter how accurate its boxes are~\cite{streaming-perception}. The streaming average precision (sAP) metric~\cite{streaming-perception} captures this by scoring each detection against the scene at its \emph{delivery} time.

Streaming perception has been studied almost entirely for a \emph{single} detector on a \emph{single} accelerator, reported as one aggregate sAP number~\cite{streamyolo,damo-streamnet,asap,longshortnet}. Modern edge platforms, however, are heterogeneous and \emph{multi-tenant}: a GPU runs the detector in FP32, an INT8 NPU runs a quantized copy at far lower power, and the GPU is frequently shared with other co-resident models. A natural deployment question is which device should run the detector, and the established way to answer it is an evaluation protocol: profile each device in isolation (one stream, no competing workload) and pick the better one. We show that this protocol can point the \emph{wrong} way, because the isolated measurement cannot see a loss that appears only when the shared GPU is contended.

\paragraph{Two opposite size-stratified losses.}
Detection quality is lost in two unrelated, size-dependent ways, on two different devices (Fig.~\ref{fig:failures}). \emph{(i)~Quantization loss.} On the INT8 NPU, low-precision arithmetic drops the low-confidence predictions of small objects below threshold. This loss is steeply size-stratified---large on small objects, negligible on large ones---and is a property of the \emph{detector and its precision}, independent of load. \emph{(ii)~Latency staleness.} On the GPU, boxes keep their full accuracy but are delivered late once a co-tenant competes for the device. A late box ages out of alignment fastest for nearby, large-appearing objects, which traverse more pixels per frame, so this loss falls hardest on \emph{medium and large} objects and is a property of the \emph{streaming clock under contention}. The two losses run in \emph{opposite} size directions, and isolated evaluation, which fixes contention to zero, sees only the quantization loss. It therefore cannot say which device the detector should use once deployed.

\paragraph{The consequence: the device choice reverses under GPU co-tenancy.}
Read in isolation the verdict is clear: the FP32 GPU wins, since the NPU only loses accuracy and gains nothing. But on a shared platform, when a GPU-resident co-tenant (\eg\ a vision--language model~\cite{mobilint-aries,qwen-vl}) saturates the device, the worst-stream sAP of every GPU camera collapses (to $0.016$ under our heaviest co-tenant) while the separate-path INT8 NPU keeps delivering; moving \emph{all} cameras to the NPU then recovers worst-stream sAP by $4$--$5\times$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). The reversal is not that the NPU is intrinsically better---in isolation it is worse---but that a fixed quantization penalty is eventually outweighed by a contention penalty isolated evaluation never imposes (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}).

\paragraph{The co-tenant type decides the flip.}
The reversal is conditional on \emph{which kind} of co-tenant shares the GPU: a light CNN triggers none, a CPU-bound language model cancels the NPU's advantage (it also slows the NPU's host post-processing), and only a GPU-saturating workload drives the flip. ``Evaluate under contention'' is therefore not enough---one must evaluate under the \emph{right} contention (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{The failure is hidden by mean sAP.}
The safety envelope of a multi-camera loop is set by its \emph{worst} camera at any instant---one blind camera is a failure regardless of the others---so the deployment-relevant quantity is the worst-stream sAP, not the mean. Under a placement that keeps some cameras on the saturated GPU and others on the NPU, the mean is buoyed by the surviving NPU streams while the worst camera has already failed. Mean sAP, which prior streaming work reports, can rank placements correctly yet badly understates how far the safety-governing worst camera has failed (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We frame this as a \emph{diagnostic}: we propose no new scheduler, but show that the accuracy signal schedulers rely on---isolated, single-camera profiling---can be wrong about device placement once the GPU is shared. This paper makes three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1: Isolated single-camera profiling mis-ranks GPU/NPU placement under GPU co-tenancy.} The FP32 GPU that wins in isolation falls well below the INT8 NPU when a GPU-resident co-tenant saturates the shared GPU; placing all cameras on the NPU recovers worst-stream sAP by $4$--$5\times$, while the placement isolated profiling prescribes---All-GPU---is the worst deployable choice under saturation (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).

\item \textbf{C2: Two size-opposed losses, causally separated, and a co-tenant-type condition for the flip.} Streaming loss decomposes into a load-stable INT8 quantization loss concentrated on \emph{small} objects and a contention staleness loss concentrated on \emph{large} objects. We separate them causally with a host-thread setting that toggles frame staleness while leaving the detections bit-identical, and we show the device flips only under GPU-saturating co-tenancy, not under light or CPU-bound co-tenants (Sec.~\ref{sec:model},~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{C3: Worst-stream sAP, not mean sAP, is the placement-relevant metric.} Worst-case-over-subgroups reporting is standard in robustness and fairness~\cite{groupdro} and in multi-stream systems~\cite{biswift,videoedge}, but streaming-perception evaluation reports the mean, which substantially \emph{understates failure severity} once some streams stay healthy while others collapse---even when it happens to rank placements the same way (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).

\end{itemize}

\section{The Reversal Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Problem statement.}
Consider a heterogeneous edge platform running $N$ concurrent camera streams, each executing its own copy of the same streaming detector, plus a co-resident background workload that competes for one device (the GPU, on our platform). By ``multi-camera'' we mean this multi-tenant case---several \emph{independent} cameras whose detectors share one accelerator complex, alongside other co-tenants---not synchronized surround-view fusion; what couples the streams is contention for shared compute. Each camera is assigned to the GPU or the NPU. The established way to decide is to profile each device \emph{in isolation}---one stream, no contention---and route each stream to whichever gives the better single-camera sAP; call this the \emph{isolated} placement. The reversal problem is that the isolated placement can be the \emph{opposite} of the one that maximizes worst-stream sAP when the same detector runs under deployment-relevant co-tenancy. Two ingredients make this non-trivial: the INT8 NPU differs from the GPU in a \emph{size-dependent} way (so the isolated verdict has a clear size signature), and shared-GPU contention adds a \emph{latency} term, itself size-dependent and opposite, that isolated evaluation never sees. Their interaction produces the reversal, which Sec.~\ref{sec:model} makes precise.

\paragraph{Related work.}
\emph{Streaming perception.} Li~\etal~\cite{streaming-perception} introduced sAP and Argoverse-HD, showing latency and accuracy must be evaluated jointly under a streaming clock; later methods improve sAP by predicting future state, jointly tuning architecture and resolution, or adapting to ego-motion~\cite{streamyolo,damo-streamnet,asap,longshortnet}. All operate on a single detector and a single accelerator and report a single aggregate (mean) sAP. \emph{Heterogeneous multi-stream edge analytics.} A large body of work shares heterogeneous platforms among video streams: NGS~\cite{ngs} routes by image complexity, and predictive-placement~\cite{etri-placement} and RL schedulers~\cite{rl-scheduler} target aggregate throughput. The systems community has long observed that profiling a workload \emph{in isolation} mispredicts its co-located behavior, motivating interference-aware provisioning~\cite{igniter}; that line, however, measures latency, throughput, and QoS---not detection accuracy---and never observes that the better \emph{device for accuracy} can reverse. \emph{Worst-case evaluation.} Reporting the worst subgroup rather than the mean is standard in robustness and fairness (group-DRO~\cite{groupdro}), and multi-stream systems pursue accuracy fairness across streams~\cite{biswift} or per-query minimum-accuracy constraints~\cite{videoedge}. We import this principle into \emph{streaming} sAP and show it \emph{reverses} the device decision a mean- or single-camera evaluation would make. \emph{Quantization and object size.} INT8 quantization is known to harm small-object detection~\cite{yolov6,tensorrt-int8,small-object-survey}, motivating quantization-aware training~\cite{quant-aware-detection}, scale-invariant losses~\cite{nwd,gcd}, and size/speed-driven model selection~\cite{tod}; this literature treats the size-stratified loss as a single-detector property to \emph{reduce}, whereas we treat it as one of two \emph{device-dependent} terms whose interaction with contention decides placement. To our knowledge, the joint treatment of streaming sAP, GPU co-tenancy, worst-stream evaluation, and the resulting placement reversal has not been characterized.

\section{A Simple Model of the Two Losses}
\label{sec:model}

We give a deliberately simple account whose purpose is to make the decomposition explicit and falsifiable: to say, per object size, which device detects best, and under what contention the device choice reverses. The unit of analysis is a camera; each device subtracts a different, size-dependent loss from the same offline accuracy ceiling.

\paragraph{Quantization loss.}
On the INT8 NPU, objects lose accuracy to quantization but are insulated from GPU-side contention, because the NPU compute path is physically separate (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion:mechanism}). This loss does not depend on GPU load. It is steeply size-stratified---large on small objects, whose fine box regression is most damaged by low precision, and \emph{negligible on large objects} (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{Latency-staleness loss.}
On the GPU, objects keep their full FP32 quality but pay a streaming-staleness penalty once a co-tenant competes for the device: detections arrive late and fall out of alignment. This latency loss is near zero when the GPU is uncontended and grows with co-tenant pressure. It too is size-stratified, but in the \emph{opposite} direction: it concentrates on medium and large objects, whose nearby boxes traverse many pixels per frame and misalign fastest when late, whereas small distant objects move little and tolerate delay. Isolated evaluation holds contention near zero and never observes this loss---which is why a decision read from it can reverse.

\paragraph{The crossover condition.}
Both devices start from the same offline accuracy ceiling $A$; the GPU pays a contention-latency loss $L(C)$ that grows with GPU contention $C$, while the NPU pays a fixed quantization loss $Q$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sAP}_{\text{GPU}} = A - L(C), \qquad \mathrm{sAP}_{\text{NPU}} = A - Q, \qquad \text{NPU preferred} \iff L(C) > Q.
\end{equation}
With the GPU uncontended, $L(C)\!\approx\!0 < Q$, so isolated evaluation prefers the GPU. As $C$ rises $L(C)$ grows; once a co-tenant \emph{saturates} the GPU, $L(C) > Q$ holds for \emph{every} camera at once and the optimal placement collapses to all-NPU. The reversal is therefore global rather than per-camera: a saturating co-tenant pushes the whole fleet across the condition together.

\paragraph{What the model does and does not claim.}
The model predicts a \emph{conditional} reversal: it occurs only when GPU contention is severe enough that the latency loss overtakes the (small) quantization loss. It does not claim the NPU is generally better---under light or no GPU contention the GPU wins, since the camera then pays only the NPU's quantization penalty. And because the latency loss is driven by whatever shares the GPU, the model predicts that the \emph{type} of co-tenant matters: only a co-tenant that actually saturates the GPU compute path triggers the flip, a prediction we test directly (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Platform.}
All experiments run on one workstation with a host x86 CPU, an NVIDIA RTX~5090 GPU, and a Mobilint MLA100 NPU (PCIe Gen4~$\times$8). The MLA100, built on Mobilint's ARIES NPU, is rated up to $80$~TOPS INT8 within $25$~W, exposes eight on-chip cores, and runs INT8 models exclusively.
GPU/CPU detection uses ONNX Runtime (CUDA/CPU); NPU detection uses the Mobilint SDK, whose \emph{single} mode gives each instance one core and supports up to eight concurrent instances. We pin host post-processing to a fixed thread count, the platform's intended operating point; we return to this setting in Sec.~\ref{sec:results:decomp}, where it doubles as a controlled lever on frame staleness.

\paragraph{Detector and dataset.}
The main experiments use YOLOv11s~\cite{yolov11}, a recent anchor-free detector that fits the $33$~ms per-frame budget on the GPU and NPU (the CPU sits at the boundary). We use public COCO-pretrained weights on Argoverse-HD without fine-tuning (its eight categories mapped to COCO), reflecting an off-the-shelf deployment; the NPU model is exported to INT8 via the Mobilint toolchain, GPU/CPU use FP32 ONNX. We evaluate on the Argoverse-HD validation split~\cite{streaming-perception} ($24$ logs at $30$~FPS from the forward-facing ring camera, the only one with streaming-sAP labels). Since our object of study is contention among \emph{independent} streams, we emulate an $N$-camera workload by replaying $N$ distinct logs concurrently---distinct scenes and size mixes---capturing shared-compute contention but not a physically synchronized rig (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations}). Per-log tertile labels (small-/medium-/large-rich, by large-object fraction) build controlled compositions.

\paragraph{Co-tenant workloads.}
We separate \emph{foreground} streams (one to eight YOLOv11s streams, whose placement we decide and quality we measure) from a \emph{co-tenant} workload pinned to the GPU. Three co-tenants span the relevant regimes: \textbf{L1$_{\text{CNN}}$} (one ResNet50~\cite{resnet}, a light GPU model; with four foreground streams the \emph{combined} load nonetheless sits near the frame boundary); \textbf{L2$_{\text{LM}}$} (ResNet50 + TinyLLaMA-1.1B~\cite{tinyllama}, which also contends for the host CPU); and \textbf{L3$_{\text{VLM}}$} (ResNet50 + Qwen2-VL-2B~\cite{qwen-vl}, a GPU-saturating companion workload~\cite{mobilint-aries}). All co-tenants run via ONNX Runtime~\cite{onnxruntime} on CUDA.

\paragraph{Metrics and placements.}
Our primary metric is streaming AP (sAP)~\cite{streaming-perception}, scoring each delivered detection against the ground truth at its delivery time; we report size-stratified sAP, and at our operating point single-stream frame skip is $\approx0$, so there is no single-stream staleness to confound it. For multi-stream runs we report mean and \emph{worst-stream} sAP, the latter our primary objective; each cell runs over a $14$~s window after a $30$-frame warm-up, averaged over three repeats. The placements we compare in the main text are \textbf{All-GPU} (the placement isolated profiling prescribes, since the GPU is at least as good as the NPU at every object size; Sec.~\ref{sec:results:single}), \textbf{All-NPU}, and \textbf{Oracle} (best worst-stream sAP over all $2^N$ placements, not deployable). Partial placements that split cameras across devices (\eg\ routing large-object-rich cameras to the NPU) do not win at the robust operating points (Sec.~\ref{sec:results:reversal}). The CPU is excluded from placement: its infer time ($31.98$~ms) sits at the frame boundary and it drops $>95\%$ of frames under any co-tenant.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We establish the three contributions in turn: the single-camera verdict (the GPU wins, C2 setup), its causal decomposition into two size-opposed losses (C2), the device reversal under GPU co-tenancy and its dependence on co-tenant type (C1), and the worst-vs-mean severity gap (C3).

\paragraph{In isolation, the GPU wins---and the loss is pure quantization on small objects.}
\label{sec:results:single}
Table~\ref{tab:single-stream} profiles each device on Argoverse-HD in isolation, at the platform's operating point where every device delivers frames within the $33$~ms budget (frame skip $\approx 0$). The INT8 NPU loses accuracy to quantization, steeply size-stratified: $-48.4\%$ relative on small objects, $-19.7\%$ on medium, and a statistically insignificant $+0.1\%$ on large. Because frame skip is $\approx0$ here, there is no single-stream staleness, so this is \emph{pure} quantization loss: it harms small objects most and leaves large objects untouched (we separate quantization from staleness directly in Table~\ref{tab:decomp}). The isolated verdict is therefore unambiguous---the GPU is at least as good as the NPU at every object size (statistically tied on large), so isolated profiling assigns \emph{every} camera to the GPU, and we take \textbf{All-GPU} as the isolated prescription. These small-object bins are dominated by safety-critical classes---pedestrians, traffic lights, signage---so the quantization loss falls on exactly the objects a control loop must detect earliest, while the largest objects are untouched. Fig.~\ref{fig:failures} shows representative frames where the INT8 NPU drops exactly these small objects while the FP32 GPU retains them.

\paragraph{The two losses, causally separated.}
\label{sec:results:decomp}
The NPU's single-stream latency is dominated not by its on-chip compute ($\approx4$~ms, faster than the GPU) but by host-side post-processing (dequantization, decoding, NMS). The number of host threads assigned to this step changes only how fast detections are \emph{delivered}---the detections themselves are bit-identical---so it is a controlled lever on frame staleness that leaves quantization untouched. Table~\ref{tab:decomp} reports the NPU$-$GPU size gap with staleness toggled off (within budget, frame skip $0$) and on (post-processing starved, frames dropped). With staleness off, the gap is pure quantization: small $-48.4\%$, large $\approx0$. Turning staleness on adds a second, \emph{oppositely} size-stratified component---negligible on small objects, $-0.098$ sAP on large---exactly the large-object penalty the model attributes to late delivery. This probe does not reproduce GPU contention itself; it isolates the streaming-clock effect of late delivery while holding the detections fixed, which is the property the GPU-side contention loss shares. The two mechanisms are thus separated by a single switch that does not touch the detector: quantization is small-biased and load-stable, staleness is large-biased and load-driven. This is the clean form of the size-opposition the rest of the paper relies on.

\paragraph{The staleness bias holds under real GPU contention, not just the thread lever.}
\label{sec:results:persize}
The thread-lever decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}) is a proxy for late delivery; we confirm the same size-structure under \emph{actual} GPU contention. Table~\ref{tab:persize-contention} reports how All-GPU's per-size sAP degrades as the GPU-only co-tenant is scaled up ($N{=}4$, $\Delta$ relative to the uncontended operating point). In the partial-contention band---the deployable regime around the crossover---the loss is sharply large-biased: at GPU frame-skip $\approx\!59\%$ large objects lose $0.095$ sAP against $0.002$ for small, the same ordering the thread lever produces (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}) but measured here directly under contention. Over the identical range the All-NPU per-size profile is contention-invariant (each size bin varies by $\le\!0.001$), consistent with the physically separate NPU compute path. Only at full saturation does the loss become size-neutral---every size bin collapses by $70$--$80\%$---so the large-object bias is the signature of the partial-contention band where the device crossover actually lives, and full saturation is the total-failure contrast.

\paragraph{Under GPU co-tenancy the device choice reverses---but only for the right co-tenant.}
\label{sec:results:reversal}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports worst-stream and mean sAP for All-GPU (the placement isolated profiling prescribes), All-NPU, and the non-deployable Oracle, at $N=4$ under the three co-tenants. The reversal appears most clearly in the VLM (bottom) row. Under the GPU-saturating VLM the GPU drops $100\%$ of foreground frames while the NPU (separate path) drops $1\%$: All-NPU scores $0.083$ worst-stream while All-GPU collapses to $0.016$---a $5.2\times$ gap---and at this saturated operating point the Oracle coincides with All-NPU. (Oracle optimizes worst-stream sAP, not mean sAP: under the VLM it places only the weakest stream on the NPU, matching the All-NPU worst-stream score while giving up some mean, so its mean in Table~\ref{tab:main} need not be the largest.) The reversal holds across stream counts under the VLM (All-NPU worst $0.083/0.083/0.083$ vs.\ All-GPU $0.018/0.016/0.014$ at $N{=}2/4/8$), so it is not an artifact of one load. The device isolated profiling preferred---the GPU---is exactly the one that fails.

The other two rows show the reversal is \emph{conditional on the co-tenant}, the discriminating variable being whether it saturates the GPU compute path. Under the light CNN co-tenant no reversal occurs and GPU-using placements stay competitive; we note that the L1$_{\text{CNN}}$, $N{=}4$ GPU figures sit near the $33$~ms frame boundary and are skip-unstable between runs (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations}), so we rest no claim on that cell. Under the CPU-bound language model the GPU stays ahead for a different reason---the LM also contends for the host CPU that runs the NPU's post-processing, raising the NPU's frame-skip to $76\%$ and cancelling its isolation advantage. Only the GPU-saturating VLM---which starves the GPU while leaving the NPU path free---produces the flip. ``Evaluate under contention'' is insufficient; one must evaluate under contention that saturates the \emph{GPU compute path} specifically. Partial placements that split cameras across devices do not win at the robust operating points: under the VLM, routing the large-object-rich cameras to the NPU scores $0.024$ worst-stream and a contention-aware split $0.015$, both far below All-NPU's $0.083$; under the LM, no split beats All-GPU. The only cell where a partial placement leads is the skip-unstable L1$_{\text{CNN}}$ near-boundary point (a contention-aware split $0.099$ vs.\ All-GPU $0.092$), which we exclude from our claim. Thus only the two extreme placements are ever the deployable best.

\label{sec:results:sweep}

\paragraph{Mean sAP understates the worst camera.}
\label{sec:results:worst}
The reversal is a \emph{worst-camera} effect, and reporting the mean conceals it. The signature is the mean-minus-worst gap in Table~\ref{tab:main}: under the VLM, All-GPU's mean ($0.047$) is $2.9\times$ its worst camera ($0.016$), because the streams differ in difficulty and the average is buoyed by the less-starved ones while the worst camera has already failed; All-NPU, which keeps every camera alive, has only a $1.5\times$ gap ($0.126$ vs $0.083$). A practitioner reporting the mean would read All-GPU as roughly three times healthier than its worst camera actually is. Mean sAP can still rank the placements correctly here---it too is highest for All-NPU---but it substantially \emph{understates the severity} of the failure, which is what a safety-critical loop must see. The gap is starker for a partial placement that splits cameras across devices: keeping some on the saturated GPU and the rest on the NPU yields mean $0.087$ but worst $0.024$ ($3.6\times$)---the surviving NPU streams hide a camera that has already failed. Per-stream sAP confirms the mechanism directly: in this split the two cameras on the NPU are immune ($0.109$ and $0.191$, unchanged as GPU load rises) while a camera left on the GPU falls from $0.115$ to $0.065$, so the worst stream is always a GPU camera and the mean is propped up by the immune NPU streams. Worst-stream sAP exposes the failure severity directly, where the mean conceals it. Argoverse-HD makes the mean especially misleading: it is dominated by small and medium objects ($>70\%$ of annotations per log), exactly the distant objects a loop must detect early, and exactly what the mean dilutes against the easy large-object majority on the surviving cameras.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Mechanism: why the reversal exists.}
\label{sec:discussion:mechanism}
The reversal rests on a hardware asymmetry. The NPU's losses are load-stable: single mode gives each instance an independent on-chip core, and the bus topology keeps the NPU compute path separate from the GPU's, so a GPU-resident co-tenant does not slow the NPU's inference. Its accuracy cost is therefore a fixed, small-biased quantization penalty (Table~\ref{tab:single-stream}). The GPU path, in contrast, accumulates a streaming-staleness loss that grows with co-tenant pressure and concentrates on large objects (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}). Because the two losses run in opposite size directions, raising GPU contention raises the GPU's loss exactly where the NPU's is smallest; once a co-tenant saturates the GPU, every GPU stream collapses and the fixed NPU penalty becomes the better deal. The one exception we observe sharpens the mechanism: a CPU-bound co-tenant (the LM) contends for the host that runs the NPU's \emph{post-processing}, so it degrades the NPU path too and the advantage cancels---confirming that the reversal requires contention localized to the GPU compute path.

\paragraph{Scope and generalization.}
\label{sec:discussion:generalization}
Both mechanisms are methodological rather than dataset-specific---the quantization loss a property of the detector and its quantized variant, the staleness loss a property of streaming-clock evaluation under shared-GPU contention---so we expect the reversal and the mean-vs-worst discrepancy to carry over to other GPU--NPU multi-camera deployments (service and warehouse robotics, retail and security) wherever the GPU hosts a saturating co-tenant. The specific thresholds (which co-tenant saturates, at what load) depend on vendor, detector, and workload and do not transfer; identifying them is exactly the per-deployment measurement we argue for.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}
The headline uses YOLOv11s; cross-capacity (YOLO11 m/l/x) and cross-architecture (non-YOLO) generality needs further INT8 NPU exports, though the account is architecture-agnostic. INT8 quantization loss is a property of the detector \emph{and} its quantization toolchain, so it depends on the toolchain version; we fix one version and characterize that state. Results come from a single reproducible operating point: the single-stream size gaps reproduce across three repeats with bit-identical detections, and the VLM reversal is large and stable (std $\le 0.001$). We note one measurement sensitivity: at $N{=}4$ under the \emph{light} co-tenant the GPU's per-frame time sits near the $33$~ms boundary, so its frame-skip (and hence the L1$_{\text{CNN}}$ All-GPU/Contention-aware figures) is unstable between runs; we therefore do not rest any claim on that cell, and the conditional pattern across co-tenants---and the large VLM reversal---does not depend on it. The NPU's single-stream latency is dominated by host-side post-processing rather than on-chip compute ($\approx4$~ms, faster than the GPU); an isolated latency profile of the NPU thus measures the host pipeline as much as the accelerator, a further reason the isolated signal is an unreliable basis for placement. Even the best placement is bounded by the NPU's INT8 loss---under saturation it keeps cameras \emph{alive} but at INT8 accuracy; raising that bound requires reducing the quantization loss itself (quantization-aware training, mixed precision). Finally, we use one NPU vendor and card, and emulate multi-camera workloads by replaying distinct single-camera logs (Argoverse-HD annotates only the forward ring camera); validating on a synchronized multi-camera benchmark with streaming labels is future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We showed that single-camera streaming-perception evaluation can \emph{reverse} the device-placement decision on a heterogeneous GPU--NPU platform once the shared GPU runs a saturating co-tenant. In isolation the FP32 GPU wins, because the INT8 NPU's only cost is a quantization loss concentrated on small objects and negligible on large ones. But when a GPU-resident vision--language model saturates the device, every GPU stream collapses while the separate-path NPU keeps delivering, and moving all cameras to the NPU recovers worst-stream sAP by $4$--$5\times$---making the isolated prescription the worst deployable choice. Crucially, the flip is conditional on the \emph{kind} of co-tenant: a light GPU workload triggers no reversal, a CPU-bound language model cancels the NPU's edge, and only a GPU-saturating workload triggers the reversal. We separated the two underlying losses causally with a staleness lever that leaves the detector untouched, confirming a small-biased quantization loss and a large-biased staleness loss in opposition, and showed that the failure is a worst-camera effect whose severity mean sAP understates. Heterogeneous multi-camera streaming detection should therefore be stress-tested under deployment-relevant co-tenancy---including GPU-saturating workloads where these are plausible in deployment---and reported by worst-stream sAP.
